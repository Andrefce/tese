\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}
\noindent
Understanding cardiac structure is fundamentally a geometric problem. The
left ventricle (LV), the chamber that pumps oxygenated blood into the body, is
a three-dimensional structure whose shape, size, and wall thickness change
continuously throughout the cardiac cycle. At end-diastole (ED), the ventricle
has filled and is at its largest; at end-systole (ES), it has contracted, so
the cavity is smallest and the muscular wall, the myocardium, is at its
thickest. The difference between these two states is what quantifies
ventricular pumping function: cavity volumes at ED and ES give the stroke
volume and ejection fraction, while the volume of the myocardium gives its
mass \parencite{bernard2018acdc}. Beyond these global indices, the geometry of
the ventricle carries diagnostic information of its own. Myocardial wall
thickness is related to the diastolic behaviour of the ventricle
\parencite{grossman1974wallthickness}, an abnormally thick wall is the defining
feature of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy \parencite{ommen2020hcm}, and
variations in ventricular shape and thickness have been associated with
structural remodelling and cardiovascular risk at the population level
\parencite{medrano2014lvshapevariation,demarvao2014atlas}.

Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is the modality of choice for
measuring these quantities. It is non-invasive, does not use ionising
radiation, and offers good contrast between blood and myocardium, which is why
it is regarded as the reference standard for ventricular volumes, mass, and
function \parencite{petitjean2011segmentationreview,bernard2018acdc}. In
routine cine acquisitions, the LV is imaged in short-axis (SAX) planes
perpendicular to its long axis, stacked from the base, the wide end where the
valves sit, towards the apex, the pointed tip, and repeated over the cardiac
cycle so that both ED and ES are captured.

The images themselves, however, do not yield clinical measurements directly.
Volumes, mass, and wall thickness are computed only after the relevant
boundaries have been delineated on every slice: the endocardium, the inner
boundary of the blood-filled cavity, and the epicardium, the outer boundary of
the myocardium. This outlining, called segmentation, is therefore the step on
which every subsequent measurement depends. Performed manually, it is
time-consuming and subject to intra- and inter-observer variability
\parencite{petitjean2011segmentationreview}, which limits both its
reproducibility and its practicality as the number of examinations grows.
These limitations have motivated a sustained effort towards automated
segmentation, driven by public benchmarks and challenges
\parencite{suinesiaputra2014collaborative,bernard2018acdc,campello2021mnms}.
The need is most evident in large cohorts such as the UK Biobank, where
thousands of subjects must be analysed consistently
\parencite{sudlow2015ukbiobank}, and where fully convolutional networks now
produce segmentations whose derived measurements approach the agreement
observed between human experts \parencite{bai2018automated}.

Segmentation, however, answers a two-dimensional question. It produces a set of
contours on separate planes, whereas the quantities of clinical interest
describe a continuous three-dimensional organ. Regional analysis, such as
reporting wall thickness according to the American Heart Association (AHA)
17-segment model \parencite{cerqueira2002segmentation}, and the construction
of population atlases that compare anatomy across subjects
\parencite{bai2015biventricularatlas,demarvao2014atlas} both require a
description of the ventricle that extends beyond the individual slices. How
that description is obtained from segmented contours is the problem addressed
in this thesis.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem}
\noindent
SAX cardiac MRI provides detailed cross-sectional information about the LV, but
does not directly provide a complete three-dimensional representation. Within
each slice the spatial resolution is relatively high, whereas the distance
between adjacent slices is substantially larger, leaving portions of the
geometry unobserved along the ventricular long axis. An anatomically
continuous structure is thus represented by a limited number of discrete
observations, and reconstructing a continuous surface from them is an
ill-posed geometric problem: many different surfaces are consistent with the
same set of slices.

This sparsity matters because the measurements motivated above are not
properties of isolated contours. Myocardial wall thickness, for instance,
varies across the ventricle, and estimating it between slices depends not
only on the accuracy of the segmentation but also on how well the underlying
three-dimensional geometry is represented. The central challenge is
therefore not the extraction of LV contours alone, but the inference of the
geometry that lies between them. A simple connection of the segmented
contours, through interpolation or surface lofting, may produce a continuous
surface, but continuity alone does not guarantee a plausible reconstruction.
Such approaches can introduce irregularities, gaps, or local distortions,
especially when the distance between slices is large. The difficulty
increases when the endocardial and epicardial surfaces are considered
together, because they must be reconstructed as a consistent pair whose
relative position keeps the myocardial wall positive and spatially coherent
in thickness, while still preserving patient-specific shape.

The problem is further constrained by the characteristics of publicly
available cardiac MRI datasets. These datasets commonly provide images and
segmentation masks for individual slices, that is, label images in which each
pixel is marked as cavity, myocardium, or background, rather than complete
three-dimensional LV meshes, surfaces made of connected triangles. Meshes are
nevertheless useful for directly training and evaluating
surface-reconstruction models because they provide an explicit representation
of the continuous geometry. A method is therefore required to bridge the gap
between sparse slice-based observations and continuous three-dimensional
surfaces.

This thesis addresses the problem with a phase-conditioned implicit neural
representation (INR), trained on synthetic LV geometries and on meshes derived
from real cardiac MRI segmentations. An INR is a neural network that
describes a surface indirectly, as the set of points where a function it
computes is zero. The proposed model receives sparse contours as input and
represents the endocardial and epicardial surfaces as continuous functions in
three-dimensional space, so that geometry can be queried at arbitrary
locations rather than only at the acquired slices. Conditioning the
representation on cardiac phase, that is, telling the model whether the
contours come from an ED or an ES frame, allows it to distinguish between the
two states, which differ markedly in cavity size and wall thickness. In
addition, the reconstruction is constrained so that the outer surface remains
outside the inner surface, preserving a positive myocardial wall thickness.

The reconstructed geometry is evaluated at both the surface and measurement
levels. Geometric agreement is assessed against reference surfaces, while
myocardial wall thickness is measured on the reconstructed geometry and
compared with measurements obtained from surfaces fitted to the same clinical
contours with a conventional smooth-surface method based on radial basis
functions (RBFs). This comparison makes it possible to evaluate not only
whether the proposed model produces visually continuous surfaces, but also
whether the resulting geometry preserves quantities relevant to cardiac
analysis. The aim is not to replace clinical interpretation, but to
investigate whether continuous geometric representations can provide a more
consistent basis for quantitative analysis of sparse cardiac MRI.

\section{Research Questions}
\label{sec:research-questions}
\noindent
This thesis addresses the following research questions:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{RQ\arabic*.}]
\item To what extent can a continuous, watertight three-dimensional surface
of an anatomical structure be reconstructed from sparse two-dimensional
contours, and how does this reconstruction compare with conventional
implicit and shape-model approaches applied to the same contours?

\item Can a single model represent multiple physiological states of the
same structure while preserving a physically plausible separation between
its inner and outer surfaces?

\item To what extent do measurements derived from the reconstructed
geometry agree with those obtained from reference clinical geometry, and
how does that agreement depend on the measurement method?
\end{enumerate}
