\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

\noindent
This chapter motivates the reconstruction of continuous left-ventricular
geometry from sparse contours delineated on cardiac magnetic resonance images
and defines the research problem. It then presents
the research questions, the contributions of the thesis, and the organisation
of the remaining chapters.

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}
\noindent
Understanding cardiac structure is fundamentally a geometric problem. The
left ventricle (LV), the chamber that pumps oxygenated blood into the body, is
a three-dimensional structure whose shape, size, and wall thickness change
continuously throughout the cardiac cycle. At end-diastole (ED), the ventricle
has filled and is at its largest; at end-systole (ES), it has contracted, so
the cavity is smallest and the muscular wall, the myocardium, is at its
thickest. The difference between these two states is what quantifies
ventricular pumping function: cavity volumes at ED and ES give the stroke
volume and ejection fraction, while the volume of the myocardium gives its
mass \parencite{bernard2018acdc}. Beyond these global indices, the geometry of
the ventricle carries diagnostic information of its own. Myocardial wall
thickness is related to the diastolic behaviour of the ventricle
\parencite{grossman1974wallthickness}, an abnormally thick wall is the defining
feature of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy \parencite{ommen2020hcm}, and
variations in ventricular shape and thickness have been associated with
structural remodelling and cardiovascular risk at the population level
\parencite{medrano2014lvshapevariation,demarvao2014atlas}.

Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is the modality of choice for
measuring these quantities. It is non-invasive, does not use ionising
radiation, and offers good contrast between blood and myocardium, which is why
it is regarded as the reference standard for ventricular volumes, mass, and
function \parencite{petitjean2011segmentationreview,bernard2018acdc}. In
routine cine acquisitions, the LV is imaged in short-axis (SAX) planes
perpendicular to its long axis, stacked from the base, the wide end where the
valves sit, towards the apex, the pointed tip, and repeated over the cardiac
cycle so that both ED and ES are captured.

The images themselves, however, do not yield clinical measurements directly.
Volumes, mass, and wall thickness are computed only after the relevant
boundaries have been delineated on every slice: the endocardium, the inner
boundary of the blood-filled cavity, and the epicardium, the outer boundary of
the myocardium. This outlining, called segmentation, is therefore the step on
which every subsequent measurement depends. Performed manually, it is
time-consuming and subject to intra- and inter-observer variability
\parencite{petitjean2011segmentationreview}, which limits both its
reproducibility and its practicality as the number of examinations grows.
These limitations have motivated a sustained effort towards automated
segmentation, driven by public benchmarks and challenges
\parencite{suinesiaputra2014collaborative,bernard2018acdc,campello2021mnms}.
The need is most evident in large cohorts such as the UK Biobank, where
thousands of subjects must be analysed consistently
\parencite{sudlow2015ukbiobank}, and where fully convolutional networks now
produce segmentations whose derived measurements approach the agreement
observed between human experts \parencite{bai2018automated}.

Segmentation, however, answers a two-dimensional question. It produces a set of
contours on separate planes, whereas the quantities of clinical interest
describe a continuous three-dimensional organ. Regional analysis, such as
reporting wall thickness according to the American Heart Association (AHA)
17-segment model \parencite{cerqueira2002segmentation}, and the construction
of population atlases that compare anatomy across subjects
\parencite{bai2015biventricularatlas,demarvao2014atlas} both require a
description of the ventricle that extends beyond the individual slices. How
that description is obtained from segmented contours is the problem addressed
in this thesis.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem}
\noindent
Reconstructing the LV in three dimensions from segmented SAX contours is a
long-standing problem in cardiac image analysis. A cine SAX stack typically
resolves the ventricle at $1$--$2$~mm within each slice, but consecutive
slices lie $6$--$10$~mm apart \parencite{petitjean2011segmentationreview}.
Segmentation therefore delineates the myocardium accurately where it is
imaged, yet leaves the geometry between slices, including the curvature of the
apex and the closure of the base, unobserved. Recovering a continuous surface
from such a stack is an ill-posed problem: many different surfaces pass
through the same contours, and the result depends on the assumptions used to
fill the gaps.

Several families of methods supply these assumptions. Contour lofting joins
corresponding points on adjacent slices and passes exactly through the
observations, but produces piecewise-linear geometry between them
\parencite{petitjean2011segmentationreview}. Statistical shape models (SSMs)
restrict the surface to the modes of variation of a training population
\parencite{cootes1995asm,frangi2002cardiacssm,heimann2009ssmreview}, which
stabilises the reconstruction when observations are sparse but can draw
atypical anatomy towards the population mean. Radial basis function (RBF)
interpolation defines the surface as the zero level set of a smooth function
fitted to the contour points \parencite{carr2001rbf}, trading regional detail
for smoothness. More recently, implicit neural representations (INRs), which
encode a shape as the set of points where a function computed by a neural
network is zero \parencite{deepsdf2019,occupancynetworks2019}, have been used
to reconstruct cardiac surfaces from anisotropic images and from sparse
segmentations \parencite{bras2026anisotropy,muffoletto2025nihc}. Closed LV
surfaces from sparse contours are therefore not new in themselves; the
approaches differ in the prior that fills the unobserved geometry, and hence
in how far a measurement on that geometry reflects the data rather than the
prior.

The difference matters most when the quantity of interest is myocardial wall
thickness. Thickness is measured between two surfaces, so the endocardium and
epicardium must be reconstructed as a consistent pair whose separation remains
positive and spatially coherent; surfaces reconstructed independently may each
be plausible and still intersect or collapse the wall between them
\parencite{bras2026anisotropy}. Priors are also often tied to a single cardiac
phase, and a shape model built from diastolic anatomy cannot describe the
contracted ventricle at ES without imposing a diastolic shape space on systolic
observations. Moreover, a discrepancy of one or two millimetres between two
reconstructions of the same contours is small relative to the cavity but
substantial relative to the wall. Surface-distance and overlap metrics, the
usual criteria for reconstruction quality \parencite{taha2015metrics}, do not
by themselves show how such discrepancies propagate into thickness
measurements.

Public data impose a further constraint. Benchmarks such as the Automated
Cardiac Diagnosis Challenge (ACDC) and the second Multi-Centre, Multi-Vendor
and Multi-Disease Cardiac Segmentation challenge (M\&Ms-2) provide images and segmentation masks for individual slices
\parencite{bernard2018acdc,martinisla2023mnms2}, that is, label images in
which each pixel is marked as cavity, myocardium, or background, rather than
complete three-dimensional LV meshes, surfaces made of connected triangles.
Without an independent three-dimensional reference, a learned model must be
supervised with surfaces fitted to the same contours and assessed only through
fidelity to the observed slices and agreement with alternative
reconstructions.

This thesis addresses the problem with a phase-conditioned INR, trained on
synthetic LV geometries sampled from an SSM and on meshes derived from real
cardiac MRI segmentations, as illustrated in \cref{fig:intro-overview}.
Rather than introducing a new representation, it adapts the implicit
formulation to the constraints described above. The proposed model takes
sparse contours as input and represents the endocardial and epicardial
surfaces as continuous three-dimensional functions that can be queried
anywhere, not only at the acquired slices. Conditioning on cardiac phase lets
one model represent both ED and ES, which differ markedly in cavity size and
wall thickness, and defining the epicardium through a positive offset from the
endocardium keeps the outer surface outside the inner one by construction.

Because no independent reference exists, the evaluation is comparative. The
reconstruction is assessed against the observed contours and against RBF and
SSM reconstructions of the same contours, including when slices are removed,
and wall thickness is measured on each geometry with several methods. This
design separates the effect of the reconstruction prior from that of the
measurement method, and asks not only whether the proposed model produces
continuous surfaces, but whether the resulting geometry preserves quantities
relevant to cardiac analysis. The aim is not to replace clinical
interpretation, but to investigate whether continuous geometric
representations can provide a more consistent basis for quantitative analysis
of sparse cardiac MRI.

\section{Research Questions}
\label{sec:research-questions}
\noindent
As \cref{sec:problem} shows, a closed LV surface can already be obtained from
sparse contours by RBF interpolation, SSM fitting, or an implicit neural model.
The open questions concern what a learned prior contributes relative to these
alternatives, whether it can satisfy the paired-surface and multi-phase
requirements of wall-thickness analysis, and how strongly the resulting
measurements depend on choices made during reconstruction and measurement.
This thesis therefore addresses the following research questions (RQs):

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{RQ\arabic*.}]
\item How does an implicit reconstruction learned from synthetic and clinical
LV geometry compare with RBF and SSM reconstructions of the same sparse SAX
contours in fidelity to the observed contours, agreement on the geometry
inferred between slices, and sensitivity to the number of available slices?

\item Can a single phase-conditioned model reconstruct paired endocardial and
epicardial surfaces at both ED and ES while maintaining a positive wall
thickness, without fitting a separate model to each phase or reconstructing
the two surfaces independently?

\item To what extent does myocardial wall thickness measured on the
reconstructed geometry depend on the reconstruction prior and on the
thickness method, and are regional and pathology-related differences in
thickness retained?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:introduction-contributions}
\noindent
The thesis makes three contributions: a shared supervision pipeline, an
adaptation of implicit reconstruction to paired LV surfaces, and an evaluation
approach that distinguishes geometric and measurement consistency.

A shared data pipeline is developed for synthetic LV shapes sampled from an
SSM and clinical ED and ES segmentations from ACDC
and M\&Ms-2. Both sources are converted into contour inputs and surface-based
supervision, allowing synthetic pre-training and clinical fine-tuning within
the same representation. The clinical surfaces are fitted to the observed
contours and used to train the model; they are not independent three-dimensional
anatomical ground truth.

The model adapts established point-set and implicit-representation methods by
summarising endocardial and epicardial contour evidence separately and
combining a global shape description with local contour information. Its
phase conditioning and directly supervised positive-offset coupling allow
these components to represent paired LV surfaces at ED and ES. The
contribution is this task-specific combination, not the underlying methods
themselves.

An evaluation approach is also established to separate reconstruction from
measurement. It examines fidelity to the observed contours, agreement with
RBF and SSM reconstructions of the same inputs, and sensitivity to slice
removal. Four wall-thickness methods are compared on the same geometry
before local measurements are summarised in AHA-17 regions and examined
across cardiac phases and patient groups. In the absence of independent
three-dimensional ground truth, these comparisons characterise consistency
and dependence on methodological choices rather than establish anatomical
accuracy or diagnostic performance.

\section{Thesis Organisation}
\label{sec:thesis-organisation}
\noindent
The remaining chapters follow the progression from the reconstruction problem
to its evaluation. \Cref{ch:literature-review} reviews cardiac MRI-based
reconstruction and myocardial wall-thickness measurement, providing the
context for the methodological choices. \Cref{ch:methodology} describes the
data preparation, the proposed model, its training and inference, and the
methods used to measure wall thickness. \Cref{ch:results} evaluates the
reconstructed geometry, its sensitivity to sparse observations, and the
resulting local and regional thickness measurements. Finally,
\cref{ch:conclusions} answers the
research questions, summarises the contributions and limitations, and
identifies directions for future work.
