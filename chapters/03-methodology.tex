\chapter{Methodology}
\label{ch:methodology}

\noindent
This chapter describes how the proposed machine learning model reconstructs
the LV in 3D from contours drawn on a few SAX slices separated by gaps, and
how myocardial wall thickness is then measured on the reconstructed surfaces.
The model learns first from synthetic hearts and then from clinical scans, and
several established thickness methods are compared before regional results
are reported.

\section{Overview}
\label{sec:method-overview}
\noindent
Wall thickness is the distance between the inner and outer surfaces of the
LV wall, the endocardium and the epicardium. Measuring it everywhere requires
both surfaces in 3D, but a SAX acquisition shows them only on a stack of
separate slices. In~\cref{fig:methodology-pipeline} (d), the blue and orange
rings mark the two boundaries on each slice, and the empty space between
consecutive rings is anatomy that was never imaged. These sparse
observations, roughly ten slices several millimetres
apart~\parencite{kramer2020cmrprotocols,bernard2018acdc}, are the starting
point: continuous surfaces must be recovered from them before thickness can
be measured between the slices.

The proposed reconstruction model fills these gaps. Moving from the rings
in~\cref{fig:methodology-pipeline} (d) to the surfaces in panel~(e), it turns
the observed boundaries into two continuous surfaces, one nested inside the
other, separately at ED and ES. To learn this, the model needs examples in
which contour rings are paired with the complete 3D shape they were taken
from. Clinical datasets cannot provide such examples, because their
annotations exist only on the slices themselves. The method therefore has to
solve two problems: how to obtain training examples, and how to reconstruct
from them.

The two upper panels of~\cref{fig:methodology-pipeline} show how the training
examples are obtained. In panel~(a), an SSM, a statistical description of how
LV shape varies across a population~\parencite{cootes1995asm,bai2015biventricularatlas}, generates synthetic ED hearts in which
the blue endocardium sits inside the orange epicardium. Slicing these hearts
produces contour rings like those of a real scan, and because the full shape
is known, every set of rings comes with its exact answer. This first stage
gives the proposed reconstruction model a geometric prior, a learned
expectation of what a plausible LV looks like, before it sees clinical data.
Panel~(b) then adds real contours from ACDC and M\&Ms-2 at both ED and ES,
with smooth surfaces fitted to them as targets. The arrow labelled ``weights''
links the two stages: fine-tuning on clinical cases starts from what was
learned on synthetic ones, and ES shapes are learned from the clinical cases
alone. Because the fitted clinical targets come from the same separated
contours, they are useful for training but do not reveal the true anatomy
between slices.


Panel~(c) shows how the proposed reconstruction model links the contours to
the two surfaces. An encoder condenses all contour points of a case into a
compact numerical description. An implicit decoder can then be asked about
any position in space, including positions in the gaps between slices, and
answers with one value per surface. For the endocardium this value is an
SDF~\parencite{deepsdf2019}:

\vspace{2mm}

(a)~Pre-training on synthetic ED surfaces and their SAX contours.
(b)~Fine-tuning on clinical ED and ES contours with fitted target surfaces.

\label{fig:methodology-pipeline}

its size is the distance from the position to the surface, and its sign
states whether the position lies inside (negative) or outside (positive). The
surface is therefore never stored directly; it is the set of positions where
the value is exactly zero, its zero level set, in the same way that a contour
line on a map joins points of equal height. The epicardial value is obtained
by subtracting a strictly positive offset from the endocardial value. Because
the outer field is built from the inner one, the two fields are coupled, and
the epicardium cannot cross inside the endocardium, which is why the blue and
orange outlines in panel~(c) always remain nested. The offset is a difference
between field values, not a measured wall thickness. At inference, the model
receives only contour points labelled by tissue and cardiac phase, so a
single model serves both ED and ES.

Reconstruction and measurement are kept separate, as the step from
panel~(e) to panel~(f) shows. In panel~(f), colour gives local wall thickness
in millimetres, on one scale shared by ED and ES. A curved wall can be
measured across in more than one reasonable way, and different rules give
different values, so four established methods are compared on the same
surfaces; the maps shown use Laplace thickness. Panel~(g) shows systolic
thickening, ES minus ED, on its own colour scale, and the bullseye
summarises the same difference for each AHA-17 segment. These panels
illustrate what the pipeline produces rather than prove that it is accurate.
Accuracy is assessed by comparing reconstructions with the observed contours
and fitted surfaces, and the method comparison decides which thickness
estimates are used in the regional analysis.

The rest of the chapter follows the figure from top to bottom.
\Cref{sec:data-preparation} builds the training data from both sources and

proposed reconstruction model and the adaptations summarised
in~\cref{tab:model-components-origin};~\cref{sec:training-inference} covers
training and the extraction of surfaces; and~\cref{sec:wall-thickness-protocol}
defines the thickness methods and the AHA-17 regional summaries.
\Cref{ch:results} then evaluates these steps against the observed contours
and fitted surfaces, under slice removal, and across cardiac phases and
patient groups, to answer the research questions
in~\cref{sec:research-questions}.

\section{Data Preparation}
\label{sec:data-preparation}
\noindent
Learning to fill the gaps between slices requires examples in which the
observed contours are paired with the closed 3D surfaces they came from. The
two branches in~\cref{fig:methodology-pipeline} (a) and~(b) obtain such pairs
from opposite ends: the synthetic branch starts from complete shapes and
slices them, whereas the clinical branch starts from slices and builds
surfaces from them. ACDC and M\&Ms-2 supply images and slice-wise segmentation
masks, label images in which every pixel is marked as cavity, myocardium, or
background, but not the closed (``watertight'') endocardial and epicardial
surfaces that a signed-distance decoder needs as supervision. The UK Digital
Heart Project LV
SSM\footnote{\url{https://github.com/UK-Digital-Heart-Project/Statistical-Shape-Model}}
fills this gap with paired endocardial and epicardial ED surfaces over a
controlled range of healthy shapes, from which 1300 synthetic ED cases were
generated for pre-training. The clinical segmentations add patient-specific
anatomy in both cardiac phases; for fine-tuning, they were converted into
closed surfaces by fitting smooth implicit functions to their contours. The
two sources are described in turn, followed by the shared format,
augmentation, and the division of patients into training, validation, and
test sets.

\subsection{Synthetic ED geometry from the statistical shape model}
\label{subsec:missing-ground-truth}
\noindent
Pre-training needs many different ventricular shapes, not repeated copies of
one. An SSM describes this variety through a population mean and its
principal modes~\parencite{heimann2009ssmreview}, mutually orthogonal
directions of variation ordered by how much of the population's departure
from that mean each one explains. Moving along the first modes changes size,
elongation, and asymmetry, as~\cref{fig:ssm-mean-modes} shows, with the blue
endocardium and the orange epicardium changing together. Sampling these
modes yields closed endocardial and epicardial surfaces with controlled
variation, which serve as exact supervision for pre-training. The samples
also have clear limits: they contain no acquisition effects, no annotation
variability, and, for the SSM used here, no systolic geometry. Synthetic
pre-training therefore teaches the proposed reconstruction model what an LV
generally looks like before it is adapted to clinical observations.

The synthetic stream draws on the UK Digital Heart Project LV SSM of~\textcite{bai2015biventricularatlas}, built from more than 1,000
high-resolution cardiac MRI scans of healthy subjects. The model describes the
ED phase only. Its mean mesh contains two closed surfaces, the endocardium and
the epicardium, with 22\,043 vertices in total (the corner points of the
triangles that make up the mesh). The mesh is accompanied by 100 principal
modes of variation and their
100 eigenvalues $\lambda_i$, each measuring how much of the population's
variation its mode explains. A new ED mesh, containing both surfaces, is
generated by
\begin{equation}
	X(b) = \bar{X} + \Phi b,
	\label{eq:ssm-generation}
\end{equation}
where $\bar{X}$ is the mean shape flattened to a vector, $\Phi$ is the matrix of
mode vectors, and $b = [b_1, \ldots, b_{100}]^\top$ is the sampled coefficient
vector. The standard deviation (SD) $\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i}$ sets the scale of
variation along mode $i$. The first modes capture the largest sources of
variation in the population: mode~1 mainly controls overall LV size, mode~2 the
elongation along the long axis, and higher modes increasingly local
deformations~(\cref{fig:ssm-mean-modes}).

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{fig_ssm_mean_modes}
	\vspace{2mm}
	\caption[LV SSM mean shape and principal modes]{UK Digital Heart Project LV
	SSM mean shape and first three PCA
	modes at $\pm3\sigma$, with the endocardium in blue and the epicardium in
	orange.}
	\label{fig:ssm-mean-modes}
\end{figure}

Of the 100 modes, the first 32 capture 99\% of the shape variance and define
the sampling space. Each candidate coefficient vector $b$ was drawn by sampling
each active component as $b_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_i^2)$ and was accepted
only if it satisfied
\begin{equation}
	\left|\frac{b_i}{\sigma_i}\right| \leq 3,
	\qquad
	\sum_i \left(\frac{b_i}{\sigma_i}\right)^2 \leq \chi^2_{0.99,d},
	\label{eq:ssm-latent-constraint}
\end{equation}
where $d$ is the number of active modes and $\chi^2_{0.99,d}$ is the 99th
percentile of the chi-squared distribution with $d$ degrees of freedom. The
first condition keeps every mode within three SDs of the mean.
The second limits the overall Mahalanobis
distance~\parencite{mahalanobis1936distance}, which measures the
combined deviation after each mode has been scaled by its population
variability. The threshold retains combinations within the 99th percentile of
the SSM distribution.

Statistical plausibility does not guarantee anatomical plausibility, so each
accepted vector is turned into a mesh with~\cref{eq:ssm-generation} and
checked once more. Meshes whose volume, surface area, or roundness
(sphericity) fall outside limits derived from the population, or whose apex
and base point the wrong way, are discarded; panel~(f)
of~\cref{fig:ssm-sampling-pipeline} contrasts an accepted shape with a
rejected outlier. Of 2048 candidates, 1300 (63\%) passed both the statistical
and the geometric checks.

Because both surfaces belong to the same mesh, a single coefficient vector
deforms the cavity and the outer wall together, and every accepted sample
already forms an ED endocardial and epicardial pair. On the mean shape, the
distance from each endocardial vertex to the epicardium has a median of
6.1~mm, as shown by the histogram in panel~(d)
of~\cref{fig:ssm-sampling-pipeline}. These pairs give the decoder a consistent
inner and outer surface during pre-training, so that the proposed
reconstruction model learns the general relation between the two LV
boundaries before it sees clinical data. As the SSM covers only end-diastole,
the synthetic stream contributes no systolic geometry, and the synthetic wall
is never used as a reference for any thickness result.

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{fig_ssm_sampling_pipeline}
	\vspace{2mm}
	\caption[Synthetic ED dataset generation from SSM sampling.]{Synthetic ED
	dataset generation, from SSM sampling to geometric supervision. Top row,
	statistical constraints: (a)~distribution of the overall-deviation
	(Mahalanobis) statistic of~\cref{eq:ssm-latent-constraint} with the
	$\chi^2_{0.99,32}$ acceptance threshold; (b)~cumulative shape variance, with
	32 active modes reaching 99\%; (c)~accepted coefficient samples in the first
	two modes; (d)~distribution of the endocardial-to-epicardial distance on the
	SSM mean shape. Bottom row, geometric construction: (e)~mesh
	synthesis via~\cref{eq:ssm-generation}; (f)~an accepted shape next to a
	rejected outlier; (g)~the 10 SAX contour levels; (h)~the 2048 near-surface
	and volumetric query points used for decoder supervision.}
	\label{fig:ssm-sampling-pipeline}
\end{figure}

Finally, each surface pair was converted into the input and supervision
required by the proposed reconstruction model.
Slicing both surfaces at 10 evenly spaced levels along the long axis produces
SAX contour rings separated by gaps, reproducing the sparse observations of
a clinical acquisition. For each case, 2048
query points, locations in space at which the decoder will be asked for a
value during training, were sampled near the surfaces and in the surrounding
volume, and the signed distance to each surface, or an inside/outside label,
was recorded at every one of them; these points provide the dense volumetric
supervision for the decoder. The lower sequence
of~\cref{fig:ssm-sampling-pipeline} connects mesh synthesis in panel~(e)
and geometric acceptance in panel~(f) to the rings in panel~(g) and query
points in panel~(h). Their different spatial distributions distinguish the
contours supplied as input from the locations used to supervise predictions
throughout the volume. The clinical stream supplies the patient anatomy on
which the proposed reconstruction model is evaluated.

\subsection{Clinical ED and ES surfaces from SAX labels}
\label{subsec:real-ed-es-datasets}
\noindent
Synthetic hearts teach general shape, but adapting the proposed
reconstruction model to real patients requires real contours, even though
these come without complete target surfaces. The gaps between the slices
in~\cref{fig:problem-visualisation} are exactly where geometry is missing, and
they must be filled before the annotations can serve as surface supervision.
The clinical data come from two public collections, ACDC~\parencite{bernard2018acdc} and M\&Ms-2~\parencite{martinisla2023mnms2}, which together provide 460 patients
(100 from ACDC and 360 from M\&Ms-2) with outlines of the cavity, the
myocardium, and the RV drawn by experts at ED and ES.

These data contribute what the synthetic stream
lacks: scanner and site variability, the way clinicians actually delineate the
wall, patient-specific and in part pathological anatomy, and the contracted
geometry of ES.

In~\cref{fig:problem-visualisation}, the blue and orange outlines identify
the endocardial and epicardial boundaries, while green marks the RV. The
images on the left show ED and ES; the ED stack on the right preserves the
physical spacing between slices. Its gaps are not removed by segmentation,
so constructing closed surfaces requires assumptions about the intervening
anatomy. The fitting method therefore determines the geometry that the
proposed reconstruction model is trained to reproduce.

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.98\linewidth]{acdc_problem_visualisation}
	\vspace{2mm}
	\caption[Clinical input for the real-data reconstruction pipeline.]{Clinical-input view used in the real-data pipeline: (a) representative
	ACDC ED/ES SAX slices with labels and (b) the corresponding ED SAX stack,
	with the physical spacing between acquired slices preserved.}
	\label{fig:problem-visualisation}
\end{figure}

Three options were available. Connecting corresponding points on consecutive
rings (lofting)~\parencite{fuchs1977contours} requires a one-to-one pairing of vertices between rings that
the data do not provide, and gives a surface made of flat facets between
slices, with a crease at every ring. Fitting the SSM would impose a population prior on every
target and, because the available SSM has ED modes only, would either force
systolic hearts into a diastolic shape space or treat the two phases
differently. Fitting a smooth implicit function to the rings has neither
drawback: it needs no vertex pairing and no atlas, and the same procedure
applies to ED and ES alike. The clinical targets were therefore built by
RBF fitting, which leaves the SSM in its role as the
source of synthetic pre-training geometry.

All segmentations are first placed in a common anatomical orientation with
their physical voxel spacing preserved, so that every case is expressed in
millimetres; the remaining file-handling steps are listed
in~\cref{tab:real-pipeline} of~\cref{app:supplementary-methods}.

On each acquired SAX slice, the cavity boundary defines the endocardial ring,
while the outer boundary of the combined cavity and myocardium defines the
epicardial ring. If a label contains several closed boundaries, as can occur
with a small detached fragment, only the largest is retained. Each ring is
resampled to 60 evenly spaced points and stored at its measured position along
the long axis. The blue and orange stacks
in~\cref{fig:real-mesh-steps} (c) and~(c$'$) distinguish the endocardial and
epicardial constraints. These points serve both as the observations supplied
to the encoder and as the constraints used to construct the target surfaces.

The endocardial and epicardial surfaces are fitted separately. For either
surface $s$, let $p_i$ be a contour point and $n_i$ its outward in-plane
normal, estimated from the local contour tangent and pointing away from the
ring centre. Following~\textcite{carr2001rbf}, a smooth function $f_s$ is
defined over the whole volume as a sum of RBFs,
smooth bumps whose value depends only on the distance to a centre $c_j$, plus
a linear term, and the surface is taken as its zero level set, the set of
points where the function is zero. The thin-plate-spline
kernel~\parencite{bookstein1989tps} $\phi$ used here produces the least bent
function that satisfies the constraints, which
suits a smooth anatomical wall. The function is
\begin{equation}
	f_s(x) = \sum_{j=1}^{K} w_j\phi(\lVert x-c_j\rVert_2)
	+ a_0 + a^\top x,
	\qquad \phi(r)=r^2\log r,
	\label{eq:clinical-rbf-field}
\end{equation}
In~\cref{eq:clinical-rbf-field}, the centres $c_j$ are the constraint points,
and the weights $w_j$, $a_0$, and $a$ are chosen so that the function satisfies
the constraints. The contour points fix where the function is zero, while
paired points placed a distance $d=2.5$~mm inside and outside each contour
point fix its sign and local scale through
\begin{equation}
	f_s(p_i)=0,
	\qquad f_s(p_i+d n_i)=d,
	\qquad f_s(p_i-d n_i)=-d.
	\label{eq:clinical-rbf-constraints}
\end{equation}
The constraints in~\cref{eq:clinical-rbf-constraints} are applied with mild
regularisation, so that the function passes close to, rather than exactly
through, the contours and is less sensitive to small irregularities in the
manual drawing.

The fitted function is continuous, so a mesh is obtained by sampling it on a
fine grid that extends slightly beyond the first and last rings, allowing the
surface to close at the base and apex. Light smoothing removes the staircase
pattern left by the grid, and marching
cubes~\parencite{lorensen1987marchingcubes} then traces the zero level set:
it visits every cell of the grid and places small triangles wherever the
function changes sign, so that the triangles join into a mesh following the
surface. Grid spacing and smoothing width are listed
in~\cref{tab:real-pipeline}. The contour overlays
in~\cref{fig:real-mesh-steps} (d) and~(d$'$) locate the acquired observations
relative to the fitted surfaces, distinguishing the constrained slice levels
from the geometry inferred between them.

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.98\linewidth]{fig_real_mesh_steps}
	\vspace{2mm}
	\caption[Clinical RBF surface-construction pipeline]{Clinical RBF
	contour-to-surface pipeline. Panel~(a) shows one acquired SAX slice with the expert
	endocardial, epicardial, and right-ventricular contours. Panel~(b) shows the
	corresponding basal, mid-cavity, and apical labels, while panels~(c) and

	Panels~(d) and~(d$'$) overlay those constraints on the zero-level surfaces
	extracted from the smoothed RBF fields, and panels~(e) and~(e$'$) show the

	\label{fig:real-mesh-steps}
\end{figure}

Each extracted mesh must form a single closed surface without holes or
handles; minor defects are repaired, and a case is excluded when a surface
cannot be recovered, its dimensions are implausible, or the epicardium does
not fully enclose the endocardium. The final surfaces
in~\cref{fig:real-mesh-steps} (e) and~(e$'$) are the targets that survive
these checks.


Both meshes are centred and scaled using the same translation and scale
factor. As for the synthetic cases, 2048 query points are sampled near the
surfaces and throughout a surrounding box, and their signed distances to
both surfaces are recorded.

These fitted surfaces are training targets, not independent anatomical
references. Their shape between slices depends on the RBF kernel,
off-surface constraints, regularisation, and extraction resolution. Agreement
with them therefore measures reproduction of the fitted geometry, not
recovery of the true anatomy. Evaluation in~\cref{ch:results} also measures
agreement with the observed contours.

\noindent
Synthetic and clinical cases come from different sources but must reach the
proposed reconstruction model in the same form. A shared sample format lets
both branches in~\cref{fig:methodology-pipeline} (a) and~(b) feed the same
reconstruction stage. Both sources also follow the same augmentation rule,
and the data are partitioned by patient.

represent acquisition and annotation

preventing modified test cases from entering training.

The clinical patients are divided into
training, validation, and test sets in a 70\,\%/15\,\%/15\,\% proportion,
rounded to whole patients and applied to patients rather than to meshes, so
that the ED and ES meshes of one patient always fall in the same set and no
information passes from the test patients into
training~\parencite{kapoor2023leakage}. The synthetic meshes
are used for pre-training only and never appear in the validation or test
sets, so every number reported in~\cref{ch:results} is computed on clinical
cases. The role of each stream in the two training stages is summarised
in~\cref{app:supplementary-methods}. The shared format allows the same
reconstruction architecture to process either source.

\section{Proposed Reconstruction Model}
\label{sec:architecture}
\noindent
Filling the gaps between slices needs two kinds of information: a sense of
the overall ventricular shape, and the detail of the contours closest to each
gap. The proposed reconstruction model gathers both from the contour points
in~\cref{fig:model-architecture} (a): a global encoder summarises the whole
case, and a local feature grid keeps nearby detail, as shown in panel~(b).
The implicit decoder in panel~(c) uses both to predict the endocardial field
and a positive offset that defines the epicardial field, giving the nested
surfaces in panel~(d). Tissue and cardiac-phase labels allow one network to
handle both surfaces and both phases.

\begin{figure}[p]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.83\linewidth]{fig_model_architecture}
	\vspace{2mm}
	\caption[Architecture of the proposed reconstruction model]{Architecture of
	the proposed reconstruction model, conditioned on cardiac phase,
	from the contours on separated SAX planes~(a) through the global and local encoder
	paths~(b) and the implicit decoder~(c) to the coupled fields and extracted
	surfaces~(d). The point cloud in~(a) and the cut surfaces in~(d) are the
	observed contours and the corresponding reconstruction of one ACDC case,
	with the epicardium opened to expose the endocardial cavity. The offset
	$\delta$ shown in the figure is a separation between field values, not a
	measured thickness.}
	\label{fig:model-architecture}
\end{figure}

Its design builds on established methods for learning from point sets and
representing shapes implicitly. Contours are treated as an unordered point set: the same small network is
applied to every point, and for each learned feature only the strongest
response across all points is kept (max-pooling), so the result does not
depend on the order in which points are listed, as in
PointNet~\parencite{qi2017pointnet}. Spatial detail lost in that summary is
recovered from a convolutional feature grid sampled at each query point,
following Convolutional Occupancy Networks~\parencite{peng2020convonet}. The
surfaces are zero level sets of signed-distance fields predicted by a network
that also receives a compact code describing the whole case (a latent code),
in the manner of DeepSDF~\parencite{deepsdf2019},
with Fourier features of the query coordinates~\parencite{tancik2020fourierfeatures} and Eikonal regularisation during
training~\parencite{gropp2020igr}.

\Cref{tab:model-components-origin} summarises the adaptations made for
LV contours separated by gaps along the long axis and for reconstruction of
the paired surfaces. The following
subsections define the inputs, encoder, and decoder.

\begin{table}[H]
	\centering

Compared with the recent cardiac implicit models reviewed in~\cref{subsec:dl-reconstruction-review}, which reconstruct from image data~\parencite{bras2026anisotropy} or from segmentations of both ventricles on separated slices
with long-axis views and a learned biventricular prior~\parencite{muffoletto2025nihc}, the proposed reconstruction model takes only LV contour points
with tissue and phase labels, and it enforces the nesting of the two surfaces
by construction.

\subsection{Inputs to the proposed reconstruction model}
\label{subsec:architecture-input}
\noindent
The input must state three things for every point: where a boundary was
observed, which surface it belongs to, and whether the heart is at ED or ES.
In~\cref{fig:model-architecture} (a), these are the points of the blue
endocardial and orange epicardial stacks. The proposed reconstruction model
receives only their coordinates, tissue labels, and cardiac phase, not MRI
intensities.

For a batch of $B$ cases processed together, the encoder receives the contour
points as the padded tensor defined in~\cref{eq:contour-input-tensor}:
\begin{equation}
	P \in \mathbb{R}^{B\times N\times5}
	\label{eq:contour-input-tensor}
\end{equation}
and a validity mask $M\in\{0,1\}^{B\times N}$ that marks which of the $N$ rows
are real points; padding only allows cases with different numbers of points to
share a batch. Each valid row is $p_i=(x_i,y_i,z_i,t_i,q_i)$: normalised
spatial coordinates, a tissue label $t_i$ that distinguishes endocardial from
epicardial points, and a phase label $q_i$ that identifies ED or ES. The
network receives neither image intensities nor a template mesh; everything it
knows about a case comes from these five values per point. Attaching the phase
to every point, rather than introducing it only at the decoder, lets both
encoders interpret the same arrangement of contours differently for a
diastolic and a systolic heart. The tissue label serves a different purpose: it
allows evidence about the cavity boundary and evidence about the outer boundary
to be summarised separately before they are combined.

\subsection{Contour encoder}
\label{subsec:contour-encoder}
\noindent
A single global summary of the contours captures the overall shape but can
lose the local detail needed between slices. The encoder therefore keeps two
representations, shown in~\cref{fig:model-architecture} (b): a global summary
on the left and a spatial feature grid on the right. The first informs the
proposed reconstruction model about the ventricle as a whole, and the second
about the contours nearest to each point.

Following PointNet~\parencite{qi2017pointnet}, the global pathway applies a
shared network with layer widths
$5\rightarrow64\rightarrow128\rightarrow256\rightarrow256$ and rectified linear unit (ReLU)~\parencite{nair2010relu}
activations to each point independently. The encoder therefore does not
depend on the order of points within a ring or between slices. For the points
$P = \{p_i\}_{i=1}^{N}$ of one case, the network assigns each point a
256-dimensional feature, as expressed in~\cref{eq:point-encoding}:
\begin{equation}
	h_i = \phi_{\mathrm{enc}}(p_i),
	\label{eq:point-encoding}
\end{equation}
where $\phi_{\mathrm{enc}}$ denotes the shared network.

Unlike standard PointNet, this pooling operation takes separate maxima for the
endocardial set $\mathcal{E}$ and the epicardial set $\mathcal{P}$, as shown in~\cref{eq:tissue-pooling}:
\begin{equation}
	z_{\mathrm{endo}} = \max_{i \in \mathcal{E}} h_i,
	\qquad
	z_{\mathrm{epi}} = \max_{i \in \mathcal{P}} h_i,
	\label{eq:tissue-pooling}
\end{equation}
where the maximum is computed independently in each feature channel. This
preserves the strongest response to each learned pattern for each boundary
before their information is combined. The resulting descriptors form a
512-dimensional vector, which is projected to the global latent code according
to~\cref{eq:latent-projection}:
\begin{equation}
	z = W_z [z_{\mathrm{endo}}, z_{\mathrm{epi}}] + b_z,
	\label{eq:latent-projection}
\end{equation}
where $z\in\mathbb{R}^{256}$ provides the decoder with a compact description
of the complete case.

Compressing all points into $z$ discards where they are. A local pathway,
following Convolutional Occupancy Networks~\parencite{peng2020convonet},
keeps this spatial information. A second shared network gives every valid
point a 32-dimensional feature, and these features are dropped into a
regular $16^3$ grid covering the case. Features falling in the same cell are
averaged, so that densely sampled regions do not dominate merely because they
contain more points.

Three small 3D convolutions then let neighbouring cells exchange
information, producing the feature volume in~\cref{eq:local-feature-volume}:
\begin{equation}
	V \in \mathbb{R}^{32\times16\times16\times16}.
	\label{eq:local-feature-volume}
\end{equation}
For a query point $x$, the local feature is a weighted average of the eight
grid cells surrounding $x$, with larger weights for the closer cells
(trilinear interpolation), as defined in~\cref{eq:local-volume-interp}:
\begin{equation}
	v(x) = \mathrm{Interp}(V, x).
	\label{eq:local-volume-interp}
\end{equation}
The global code $z$ is shared by every query in a case, whereas
$v(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{32}$ describes the contour evidence near $x$. The grid is
deliberately coarse because a few hundred contour points cannot support a fine
volume. Its purpose is to provide local context to the decoder, not to define
the reconstruction resolution.

\subsection{Implicit decoder with positive-offset coupling}
\label{subsec:monotone-epi-decoder}
\noindent
Each surface is predicted as the zero level set of a field defined over the
whole volume, negative inside the surface and positive outside it, in the
manner of DeepSDF~\parencite{deepsdf2019}. With two surfaces to predict, the
obvious design gives the decoder in~\cref{fig:model-architecture} (c) one
independent output per field. That design leaves the relative position of the
two surfaces unconstrained precisely where the reconstruction is least
informed. 

On an acquired SAX plane, both surfaces are held in place by the
observed contours; in the gaps between planes, each is inferred on its own,
and nothing prevents them from approaching, touching, or crossing.

\Cref{fig:offset-coupling} (a) illustrates the failure. Both surfaces pass
through every contour point, yet between two planes the epicardium meets the
endocardium on one wall and dips inside it on the other. Along a ray through
the crossing, \cref{fig:offset-coupling} (b) exposes the resulting
contradiction: an interval in which $f_{\mathrm{endo}}<0$ and
$f_{\mathrm{epi}}>0$, whose points are classified as inside the cavity and
outside the epicardium at once, a wall of negative thickness. Because the
defect lies away from the observed planes, agreement with the input contours
cannot reveal it, and training against complete target surfaces can make it
less likely without excluding it for an unseen case. The proposed
reconstruction model therefore excludes it through the structure of its
outputs.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_offset_coupling}
	\caption[Coupling of the endocardial and epicardial fields]{Independent
	and coupled output fields. (a)~Schematic of two independently predicted
	surfaces that match every contour yet touch or cross between SAX planes;
	(b)~fields along the ray in~(a). (c)~Fields predicted by the proposed
	reconstruction model for one ACDC case at ED, with the epicardium at
	$f_{\mathrm{endo}}=\delta$; (d)~fields along the ray in~(c). Values
	in~(c) and~(d) are smoothed with a 2~mm Gaussian for display.}
	\label{fig:offset-coupling}
\end{figure}

Both fields are evaluated by a single coordinate network. A
network fed with plain coordinates tends towards overly smooth fields, so each
query point is first expanded with Fourier features~\parencite{tancik2020fourierfeatures}, which add sine and cosine components at
several spatial frequencies. For $x\in\mathbb{R}^3$, the encoding is defined
in~\cref{eq:fourier-encoding}:
\begin{equation}
	\gamma(x) = [x, \sin(2^0\pi x), \cos(2^0\pi x), \ldots,
	\sin(2^{L-1}\pi x), \cos(2^{L-1}\pi x)],
	\label{eq:fourier-encoding}
\end{equation}
with $L=3$, giving $3+6L=21$ components. The encoded coordinate is concatenated
with the global code to form the decoder input in~\cref{eq:decoder-input}:
\begin{equation}
	h_{\mathrm{in}}(x)=[z,\gamma(x)]\in\mathbb{R}^{277}.
	\label{eq:decoder-input}
\end{equation}
The decoder itself is a deep fully connected network of width 512 with
smooth activations, which keep the predicted fields smooth and differentiable.
Halfway through, the input vector is fed in again (a skip connection, as in
DeepSDF~\parencite{deepsdf2019}), so that
the deeper layers still see the case code and the position directly. The
local feature $v(x)$ is supplied at several points of the decoder, including
the offset head described next, so that the prediction at each position
depends on the nearby contours as well as on the global shape. The exact
layer configuration is listed in~\cref{tab:model-hyperparameters}
of~\cref{app:supplementary-methods}.

The coupling acts at the output stage, where the proposed reconstruction model
departs from an ordinary implicit decoder. The final hidden representation
feeds two output layers, or heads: one predicts the endocardial
signed-distance value $f_{\mathrm{endo}}(x)$, the other an offset $\delta(x)$
through a final function that can only return positive values. The epicardial
field is not predicted separately but derived from both outputs
through~\cref{eq:monotone-epi}:
\begin{equation}
	f_{\mathrm{epi}}(x) = f_{\mathrm{endo}}(x) - \delta(x),
	\qquad \delta(x)>0.
	\label{eq:monotone-epi}
\end{equation}
The guarantee follows in one step. Wherever $f_{\mathrm{endo}}(x)\leq0$, that
is, in the cavity or on the endocardium, \cref{eq:monotone-epi} gives
$f_{\mathrm{epi}}(x)\leq-\delta(x)<0$, so the point lies strictly inside the
epicardium. The interval of~\cref{fig:offset-coupling} (b) cannot arise, and
the outer surface can neither touch nor cross the inner one, which rules out
wall collapse by construction. Geometrically, the epicardium is the set of
points where $f_{\mathrm{endo}}=\delta$. It is therefore not a second,
independent surface but a positive level set of the endocardial field, whose
level varies from point to point with the offset.

\Cref{fig:offset-coupling} (c) shows this structure in the fields predicted for
an ACDC case whose SAX planes lie 10~mm apart. The epicardium runs among the
outer level sets of $f_{\mathrm{endo}}$ and remains separated from the
endocardium along the whole section, including the gaps in which no contour
constrains either surface. On a ray placed halfway between two planes,
\cref{fig:offset-coupling} (d) shows the same property in profile:
$f_{\mathrm{epi}}$ stays below $f_{\mathrm{endo}}$ along the entire ray, so
the endocardial zero crossing always precedes the epicardial one.

Two qualifications apply. First, the constraint orders the two fields but does
not by itself guarantee an anatomically valid surface or a valid mesh once the
fields have been sampled on a grid, which is why the extracted meshes are
checked and repaired in~\cref{subsec:inference-offset-extraction}. Second,
$\delta$ is not a wall thickness. In~\cref{fig:offset-coupling} (d), the offset
is the vertical separation between two field values at the same point,
whereas the wall is the horizontal separation between the two zero crossings.
The offset is also defined throughout the volume: at the centre of the cavity
it exceeds 20~mm, although the wall along the same ray spans less than 10~mm.
The wall-distance loss of~\cref{subsec:training-data-mix} relates $\delta$ to
the wall only at endocardial points, and thickness is measured on the
extracted surfaces with the methods of~\cref{sec:wall-thickness-protocol}.

\section{Training and Inference}
\label{sec:training-inference}
\noindent
Surface targets are needed to learn the reconstruction, but not to use it.
In~\cref{fig:methodology-pipeline}, the upper branches supply this
supervision, whereas the patient sequence below starts from contours alone.
Synthetic pre-training and clinical fine-tuning share the same architecture
and loss. Once training is complete, the predicted fields are turned into
meshes, on which reconstruction quality and wall thickness are evaluated.

\subsection{Two-stage training and loss}
\label{subsec:training-data-mix}
\noindent
Training proceeds in two stages. Starting with complete synthetic ED surface
pairs gives the proposed reconstruction model a geometric prior before it
meets the variability of clinical contours, and the transfer of weights
from~\cref{fig:methodology-pipeline} (a) to~(b) carries that prior into the
clinical stage. The first stage uses only the 1300 synthetic ED cases, whose
controlled geometry allows stronger augmentation of the separated contour
rings. Fine-tuning then uses the ACDC and M\&Ms-2 cases of both phases,
adapting the proposed reconstruction model to clinical contour quality and to
systolic anatomy. The
patient-level split and the augmentation rule of~\cref{subsec:shared-dataset-assembly} apply unchanged.

The proposed reconstruction model is conditioned on cardiac phase rather
than trained separately for ED and ES. Because the SSM provides ED geometry
only, a separate ES reconstruction model could not have received equivalent
pre-training. A shared reconstruction model allows ES cases to benefit from
the prior learned on the synthetic ED corpus, while the phase label identifies
the cardiac configuration. The RBF-derived targets are constructed identically in both phases, so
no second asymmetry is introduced at fine-tuning. The cost is that phase
information is coarse, a binary label rather than a position in the cardiac
cycle, and that the prior remains biased towards diastolic shape.

The objective combines four established implicit surface losses with a wall
loss specific to the coupled outputs of the proposed reconstruction model.
Following~\textcite{gropp2020igr}, $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{surf}}$ guides the
reconstructed boundaries towards the target surfaces. Across the sampled
volume, $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SDF}}$ matches predicted and target signed
distances, adapting DeepSDF supervision~\parencite{deepsdf2019}.
Eikonal regularisation, $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{eik}}$, requires each field to
change by one unit per unit of distance travelled, the defining property of a
true distance, so that the fields behave locally as distances to a
surface~\parencite{gropp2020igr}, while
$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{off}}$ discourages extra surfaces where no target
surface exists, following SIREN~\parencite{sitzmann2020siren}. To supervise
wall separation, $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{WT}}$ matches the field offset
$\delta$ to the target distance between the boundaries, using a scaled
Smooth-L1 penalty from Fast R-CNN~\parencite{girshick2015fastrcnn}, which is
quadratic for small errors and linear for large ones, so that a few large
errors do not dominate training.

Formally, let $\mathcal{E}$ and $\mathcal{P}$ denote sampled endocardial and
epicardial surface points, $\mathcal{Q}$ the stored query points,
$\mathcal{R}$ the union of near-surface and free-space points used for the
gradient term, and $\mathcal{F}$ the free-space subset, sampled away from both
surfaces. At each
$q\in\mathcal{Q}$, $y_{\mathrm{endo}}(q)$ and $y_{\mathrm{epi}}(q)$ are the
stored signed-distance targets, and at each $x\in\mathcal{E}$ the wall target
$t(x)=-y_{\mathrm{epi}}(x)$ is the distance from that endocardial point to the
epicardial surface. Writing $\mathbb{E}$ for the mean over the indicated set
of points, the five terms are defined in~\cref{eq:training-loss-terms}:
\begin{align}
	\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{surf}} ={}&
	\mathbb{E}_{x\in\mathcal{E}}[|f_{\mathrm{endo}}(x)|]
	+ \mathbb{E}_{x\in\mathcal{P}}[|f_{\mathrm{epi}}(x)|], \notag \\
	\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SDF}} ={}&
	\mathbb{E}_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}[|f_{\mathrm{endo}}(q)-y_{\mathrm{endo}}(q)|]
	+ \mathbb{E}_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}[|f_{\mathrm{epi}}(q)-y_{\mathrm{epi}}(q)|], \notag \\
	\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{eik}} ={}&
	\mathbb{E}_{x\in\mathcal{R}}[(\lVert\nabla_x f_{\mathrm{endo}}(x)\rVert_2-1)^2]
	+ \mathbb{E}_{x\in\mathcal{R}}[(\lVert\nabla_x f_{\mathrm{epi}}(x)\rVert_2-1)^2], \notag \\
	\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{off}} ={}&
	\mathbb{E}_{x\in\mathcal{F}}[e^{-\alpha|f_{\mathrm{endo}}(x)|}]
	+ \mathbb{E}_{x\in\mathcal{F}}[e^{-\alpha|f_{\mathrm{epi}}(x)|}], \notag \\
	\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{WT}} ={}&
	\mathbb{E}_{x\in\mathcal{E}}[\ell_{\beta_{\mathrm{WT}}}(\delta(x)-t(x))],
	\label{eq:training-loss-terms}
\end{align}
where $\alpha=5$ and $\ell_{\beta}$ is the scaled Smooth-L1 penalty with
transition parameter $\beta$, defined in~\cref{eq:wall-smooth-l1}:
\begin{equation}
	\ell_{\beta}(a)=
	\begin{cases}
		a^2/(2\beta), & |a|<\beta, \\
		|a|-\beta/2, & |a|\geq\beta,
	\end{cases}
	\qquad \beta_{\mathrm{WT}}=0.02.
	\label{eq:wall-smooth-l1}
\end{equation}
All distances are expressed in the normalised coordinate system of each case,
and the terms are combined into the complete objective in~\cref{eq:training-objective}:
\begin{align}
	\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}} ={}&
	\lambda_{\mathrm{surf}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{surf}} +
	\lambda_{\mathrm{SDF}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SDF}} +
	\lambda_{\mathrm{eik}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{eik}} +
	\lambda_{\mathrm{off}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{off}} +
	\lambda_{\mathrm{WT}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{WT}},
	\label{eq:training-objective}
\end{align}
where the weights $\lambda$ balance the five terms; their values are reported
in~\cref{tab:loss-terms} of~\cref{app:supplementary-methods}.

Optimisation uses AdamW~\parencite{loshchilov2019adamw}, a standard
gradient-based method, with a learning rate that follows cosine annealing
with warm restarts~\parencite{loshchilov2017sgdr}: it decreases along a cosine
curve and is reset at intervals, which helps training leave poor solutions.
Pre-training lasted approximately 200 epochs (passes over the training set);
fine-tuning then ran for 776 epochs, and the checkpoint at epoch 695 was
selected by validation loss. Learning rate, batch size, precision, and the
remaining settings are collected in~\cref{tab:training-config}.

\subsection{Inference, surface extraction, and comparators}
\label{subsec:inference-offset-extraction}
\noindent
Before reconstruction quality or wall thickness can be assessed, the predicted
fields must be turned into meshes, which produces the surfaces
in~\cref{fig:methodology-pipeline} (e) from the contours in panel~(d).
The encoder computes $z$ and $V$ from the contours once, and
the decoder is evaluated on a regular $96^3$ grid of points. Marching cubes
extracts the zero level sets of $f_{\mathrm{endo}}$ and $f_{\mathrm{epi}}$ from
this grid as the raw endocardial and epicardial meshes~\parencite{lorensen1987marchingcubes}. The triangle mesh is therefore an approximation chosen
at inference time, not a property of the architecture, and could be extracted
at any resolution because the fields themselves are continuous. Small
defects in the raw meshes, such as stray fragments or holes, are repaired
before evaluation, so the reported metrics describe a cleaned mesh rather
than the untouched decoder output.

The proposed reconstruction model is compared with two reconstructions built from exactly the same
contour rings. These comparators embody different and well-understood
priors. The first is an RBF implicit fit obtained with the procedure of~\cref{subsec:real-ed-es-datasets}: it interpolates the rings through a smooth
field and belongs to the same family as the fine-tuning targets. The second is
a fit of the UK Digital Heart Project shape model to the rings. The mean
shape is first placed over the rings by matching their long axis, the line
through the ring centres, together with their scale and their rotation about
that axis. The fit then repeats three steps: each ring point is matched to
its nearest SSM vertex, the SSM is re-aligned to the rings by translation,
rotation, and scale, and the first 25 mode coefficients are re-estimated
while being kept within three SDs. This scheme combines iterative closest
point registration~\parencite{besl1992icp} and least-squares similarity
alignment~\parencite{umeyama1991similarity} with the constrained shape
update of active shape models~\parencite{cootes1995asm}. As the published SSM
provides ED
modes only, this comparator exists at ED alone. Both pass through the same
repair and smoothing as the output of the proposed reconstruction model.

None of the three reconstructions is treated as ground truth. Evaluation
compares their distances from the input contours and their pairwise surface
and volume agreement. These comparisons assess reconstruction consistency,
not the contribution of individual components of the proposed reconstruction model.

\section{Wall-Thickness Evaluation}
\label{sec:wall-thickness-protocol}
\noindent
A reconstructed wall has no single local thickness until a rule is chosen for
measuring across it. On a curved wall that thins towards the apex, taking the
nearest point, travelling perpendicular to the surface, or following a smooth
path from one surface to the other can each give a different distance. The
colour maps in~\cref{fig:methodology-pipeline} (f) are therefore produced by a
separate measurement step applied to the surfaces of panel~(e); they are not
direct outputs of the proposed reconstruction model. 

Four established methods are compared on the same reconstructed surfaces. The segmentation volume
supplies only the contour rings and the orientation of the AHA coordinate
system; thickness itself is measured on the reconstructed surfaces. The
method whose measurements prove most consistent in~\cref{ch:results} is then
used for the regional analysis. The aim is to compare measurement methods and
produce 3D thickness maps, not to assess diagnostic performance for
infarction, scarring, or hypertrophy.

\subsection{Selected thickness methods}
\label{subsec:thickness-methods}
\noindent
Each method estimates the distance across the same wall, but follows a
different rule for defining it. The straight distances, curved paths, and ray
fan in~\cref{fig:wall-thickness-methods} illustrate these choices on a common
cross-section. The four methods span the main families identified in~\cref{subsec:thickness-review}: distance fields computed on a voxel grid, rays
cast from one surface to the other, and methods based on PDEs, which solve an equation over the
myocardium to obtain a smooth field running from one surface to the other
and then measure along it~(\cref{tab:wall-thickness-methods}). None is assumed in advance to be
the best. Each returns a local thickness in millimetres at the endocardial
vertices or at points projected onto the endocardium, and the resulting fields
are summarised by their mean, median, SD, 5th and 95th
percentiles, fraction of valid values, and runtime, and shown as
three-dimensional colour maps and as AHA-17 bullseye
plots~\parencite{cerqueira2002segmentation}, circular diagrams
with one cell per segment.

\begin{table}[H]
	\centering
	\caption[Wall-thickness methods selected for evaluation.]{Wall-thickness methods selected for evaluation on the
	LV geometry produced by the proposed reconstruction model, with the EDT
	boundary sum kept as the simplest baseline.}
	\label{tab:wall-thickness-methods}
	\small
	\begin{tabular}{p{0.21\textwidth} p{0.13\textwidth} p{0.56\textwidth}}
		\toprule
		Method & Category & Main measurement principle \\
		\midrule
		Laplace field & PDE & Transmural field lines from a harmonic potential \\
		Yezzi--Prince & PDE & Eulerian transport equations along the transmural field \\
		SDF cone rays & Ray / SDF & Median of multiple cone rays around the surface normal \\
		EDT boundary sum & Volumetric & Sum of endocardial and epicardial distance transforms \\
		\bottomrule
	\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\begin{figure}[tbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_wall_thickness_methods}
	\vspace{2mm}
	\caption[Comparison of wall-thickness methods.]{Conceptual comparison of the four wall-thickness methods on a
	single myocardial cross-section (endocardium in blue, epicardium in orange).
		(a) EDT boundary sum: distances from an interior point to each boundary are
		added. (b) Laplace field: curved paths of a potential rising from 0 to 1
		cross its level lines (dashed) at right angles, and thickness follows from
		the gradient magnitude. (c) Yezzi--Prince: travel distances $u$ and $v$ from
		opposite boundaries along the same field are summed. (d) SDF cone rays: the
		median distance of a fan of rays around the endocardial normal is kept
		(highlighted), limiting the effect of any single ill-oriented ray.}
	\label{fig:wall-thickness-methods}
\end{figure}

The straight arrows from each interior point
in~\cref{fig:wall-thickness-methods} (a) identify independent distances to the
two nearest boundaries. EDT boundary sum adds these distances and is the
simplest method. A Euclidean distance transform~\parencite{saito1994edt} assigns to every voxel of the myocardium its straight-line
distance to the nearest point of a given boundary; the method computes one
such transform for each surface and adds them, as defined in~\cref{eq:edt-thickness}:
\begin{equation}
	t^{\mathrm{EDT}}(x) = D_{\mathrm{endo}}(x) + D_{\mathrm{epi}}(x),
	\label{eq:edt-thickness}
\end{equation}
where $D_{\mathrm{endo}}$ and $D_{\mathrm{epi}}$ are the distance transforms
of the endocardium and the epicardium. The sum is exact for two parallel flat
walls, but it is limited by voxel size and slightly overestimates thickness
where the wall is strongly curved, because the two nearest points do not lie
on a common path across the wall.

In~\cref{fig:wall-thickness-methods} (b), the curved paths cross dashed lines
of equal potential at right angles, defining a direction through the wall
rather than two independent nearest-boundary distances. The Laplace field
method constructs this smooth field by defining a scalar potential $\psi$
over the myocardium and solving Laplace's
equation with the boundary conditions in~\cref{eq:laplace-thickness}:
\begin{equation}
	\nabla^2 \psi = 0,
	\qquad
	\psi|_{\mathrm{endo}} = 0,
	\qquad
	\psi|_{\mathrm{epi}} = 1,
	\label{eq:laplace-thickness}
\end{equation}
so that $\psi$ rises smoothly from 0 on the endocardium to 1 on the
epicardium, each value being the average of its neighbours (a harmonic
field), and acts as a potential whose field lines run across the wall.
Thickness is then estimated from the magnitude of
the gradient of $\psi$, the rate at which the field changes across the wall,
which is small where the wall is thick and large where it is thin. The paths
that follow the gradient never cross one another, which is why the method,
introduced for the thickness of the brain cortex by~\textcite{jones2000laplace},
is widely used for layered tissue; its weakness is that the numerical estimate
can degrade where the wall is thin or the gradient is small.

The opposing arrows in~\cref{fig:wall-thickness-methods} (c) represent
distances accumulated from each boundary along the same field. Their sum
defines the thickness, rather than estimating it from the gradient magnitude.
The Yezzi--Prince
method~\parencite{yezzi2003thickness} removes this weakness by computing,
along the same field, the distance travelled from each boundary with a pair of
transport equations, which accumulate path length step by step while moving
along the field and are solved directly on the voxel grid. Their simplified form is given in~\cref{eq:yezzi-prince-thickness}:
\begin{equation}
	\nabla \psi \cdot \nabla u = -1,
	\qquad
	t = u + v,
	\label{eq:yezzi-prince-thickness}
\end{equation}
where $u$ and $v$ are the distances travelled from the two boundaries and
their sum is the thickness.

The ray fan in~\cref{fig:wall-thickness-methods} (d) addresses sensitivity to
a single surface normal by testing several nearby directions. Each valid ray
connects an endocardial point to an epicardial intersection, and the
highlighted ray marks the median distance retained. Following the shape
diameter function of~\textcite{shapira2008sdf}, which estimates the local
thickness of a 3D object from several rays cast into its interior, $K$
rays are cast within a cone of half-angle $30^{\circ}$ around the normal, and
the median of the valid intersection distances is kept, as defined in~\cref{eq:cone-ray-thickness}:
\begin{equation}
	t_i^{\mathrm{cone}} = \operatorname{median}_{k=1}^{K} d_{ik},
	\qquad K=7.
	\label{eq:cone-ray-thickness}
\end{equation}
As a result, the estimate remains approximately transmural, measuring across rather than
along the wall, while being more robust than a single normal ray.

What is specific to this work is not the methods themselves but how they are used: all
four are run on the same reconstructed surfaces under the same conditions, and
their agreement with one another, rather than with a reference that does not
exist, decides which one carries the regional analysis.

\subsection{Regional AHA-17 mapping}
\label{subsec:aha17-mapping}
\noindent
Comparing local thickness across patients requires dividing every ventricle
into the same anatomical regions. The AHA 17-segment
model~\parencite{cerqueira2002segmentation}
provides this partition for clinical reporting, linking the surface patches
in~\cref{fig:aha17-cut} to matching bullseye cells. Their colours identify
segments rather than thickness values, allowing the local map from the
selected method to be summarised within each region. This segmentation scheme
was defined for two-dimensional imaging and divides the ventricle along its
long axis into basal, mid-cavity, and apical thirds. A representative SAX
slice from each third is partitioned into angular sectors around the cavity:
six sectors in the basal and mid-cavity regions and four in the apical region.
The sectors are numbered from the anterior wall, with the septal segments
facing the RV, while the apical tip forms the seventeenth segment.
In routine use, an observer measures the thickness of each segment on the
corresponding slice.

The local thickness map contains many more values than a regional report
needs. Assigning those values to anatomically oriented regions allows the
same parts of the ventricle to be compared across cases and cardiac phases,
while retaining the full surface map for more detailed inspection.

Transferring this definition to a reconstructed surface requires only that
every vertex receive the two coordinates the definition relies on, a position
along the long axis and an angle around it; the segments then become patches
of the three-dimensional surface rather than readings on single slices. The
long axis is taken as the direction of the SAX stack. The base and apex are
the first and last slices that contain myocardium, the base being the end
whose cavity cross-section is larger, and every endocardial vertex is given a
normalised position between 0 at the base and 1 at the apex. Vertices in the
first third of this range form the basal ring, those in the second third the
mid-cavity ring, those between two thirds and 0.85 the apical ring, and those
beyond 0.85 the apical cap, which is segment 17.

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_aha17_cut}
	\vspace{2mm}
	\caption[AHA-17 partition]{AHA-17 partitioning of the reconstructed LV
	surface and corresponding bullseye representation.}
	\label{fig:aha17-cut}
\end{figure}

The angle of each vertex is measured in the SAX plane around the centre of the
endocardial vertices. Its orientation is fixed by the right-ventricular label:
the direction from the LV centre to the right-ventricular centre identifies
the septum, and the anterior reference lies a quarter turn from it. The basal
and mid-cavity rings are divided into six sectors of $60^{\circ}$, while the
apical ring is divided into four sectors of $90^{\circ}$. These divisions form
segments 1--6, 7--12, and 13--16, respectively~(\cref{fig:aha17-cut}).

Before assigning the segments, a quality-assurance view displays the detected
base, apex, and septal direction. The rings are then unrolled onto concentric
circles to form the bullseye, with the basal ring outermost and the apical cap
at the centre. Each cell therefore summarises the vertices of one surface
patch. Within each segment, the mean and percentiles provide complementary
regional summaries rather than relying on a single statistic. These summaries
complement rather than replace the per-vertex maps.
