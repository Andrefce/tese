\chapter{Conclusions}
\label{ch:conclusions}

\noindent
This thesis investigated whether continuous three-dimensional left-ventricular
(LV) geometry can be reconstructed from sparse short-axis (SAX) contour
observations using an implicit neural representation. The proposed pipeline
combines synthetic pre-training based on a statistical shape model (SSM) with
fine-tuning on clinical contour observations and segmentation-derived surrogate
geometry. A phase-conditioned signed-distance decoder then reconstructs coupled
endocardial and epicardial surfaces at end-diastole (ED) and end-systole (ES).
The resulting surfaces support local and regional computational estimates of
myocardial wall thickness. The findings show that the pipeline can transform
sparse clinical observations into continuous paired surfaces and represent the
expected differences between the cardiac phases. The reconstructed geometry also
supports exploratory wall-thickness analysis across the LV. Together, these
outcomes answer the research questions at a technical level and demonstrate the
potential of implicit representations for completing sparse cardiac imaging
data.

The work should nevertheless be interpreted within its scope. The clinical
reference geometry was derived from image segmentations and was not an
independent anatomical ground truth. Surface repair and post-processing also
contributed to the final meshes, while the wall-thickness values were
computational estimates rather than clinical measurements. The comparison
between normal and hypertrophic cases was descriptive, and no competing
reconstruction methods or architectural ablations were included. The results
therefore support geometric consistency and methodological feasibility, but do
not establish clinical validity or diagnostic performance.

Future work should evaluate the approach against withheld acquired contours and
higher-resolution or independently annotated anatomical data. Comparisons with
contour lofting, volumetric interpolation, and SSM fitting
would clarify the value of the proposed reconstruction strategy. Ablation studies
could also determine the individual effects of synthetic pre-training, phase
conditioning, and coupled surface prediction. Larger and more diverse cohorts
would allow the behaviour of the method across pathology groups to be studied
more reliably. Extending the model to enforce temporal consistency throughout
the cardiac cycle would provide a more complete representation of ventricular
motion and function.

In conclusion, this thesis provides a foundation for reconstructing continuous,
patient-specific LV geometry from routinely available sparse imaging data. The
proposed pipeline offers a basis for further evaluation of three-dimensional
cardiac reconstruction and computational wall-thickness analysis.
