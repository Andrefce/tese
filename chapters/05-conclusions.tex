\chapter{Conclusions}
\label{ch:conclusions}

\noindent
This chapter closes the thesis. It summarises what was built and why, answers
the three research questions of \cref{sec:research-questions} on the evidence
of \cref{ch:results}, states the contributions, delimits the claims, and sets
out the work that should follow.

\section{Summary of the Work}
\label{sec:conclusion-summary}
\noindent
Sparse SAX cardiac MRI presents a geometric limitation.
Each slice observes the LV finely, at an in-plane pixel size
below $2$~mm, but the slices lie several millimetres apart, so the
three-dimensional wall on which clinical measurement depends is never observed
directly and has to be inferred. That inference was treated as the learning of a
continuous signed-distance representation. A phase-conditioned implicit model
was pre-trained on synthetic shapes sampled from an SSM and fine-tuned on ED and ES surfaces fitted
to ACDC and M\&Ms-2 segmentations. The model reads the sparse contour rings
through a global PointNet pathway and a local feature grid, predicts the
endocardial signed-distance field, and defines the epicardial field as that
field minus a strictly positive, directly supervised offset, so the two surfaces
cannot cross. Its output was compared with an RBF
implicit fit and an SSM fit of the same rings, and myocardial wall thickness was
measured on all three geometries with four local methods, summarised across
the AHA 17 segments, and compared between normal and HCM patients.

\section{Answers to the Research Questions}
\label{sec:conclusion-research-questions}
\noindent
\textbf{RQ1} asked how a reconstruction learned from synthetic and clinical
geometry compares with RBF and SSM reconstructions of the same sparse contours.
The model follows its input contours to within about one in-plane pixel
($1.50$~mm on the endocardium and $1.96$~mm on the epicardium), lies closer to
the RBF fit than to the SSM fit, and, unlike the SSM fit, keeps a similar
contour distance on normal and HCM cases. The three methods agree on the cavity
but not on the wall, and removing slices degrades all three by similar amounts.
The learned reconstruction therefore performs comparably to conventional
fitting and handles abnormal anatomy more evenly than the population model, but
it is not more robust to slice removal.

\textbf{RQ2} asked whether a single phase-conditioned model can reconstruct
paired endocardial and epicardial surfaces at ED and ES while maintaining a
positive wall thickness. The answer is a qualified yes. One network
reconstructs both phases, the epicardial field cannot cross the endocardial
field by construction, and the reconstructed wall thickens in every AHA
segment, from $9.21$~mm at ED to $13.87$~mm at ES. Pointwise separation on the
extracted meshes and accuracy at ES were not verified.

\textbf{RQ3} asked how strongly wall thickness depends on the reconstruction
prior and the measurement method, and whether regional and pathology-related
differences are retained. Both factors matter. The prior mainly shifts the
absolute scale while largely preserving the ranking of patients, and a
straight-ray measurement departs strongly from methods that follow the curved
wall. Absolute thickness values are therefore comparable only within the same
reconstruction and measurement pipeline. The reconstructed geometry does retain
group differences: the HCM group is $3.67$~mm thicker than the normal group in
patient-level mean ED thickness, whereas relative systolic thickening does not
separate the groups. With ten HCM patients, this is a descriptive result rather
than a diagnostic one.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:conclusion-contributions}
\noindent
This work makes three contributions. First, a shared data pipeline combines
synthetic SSM meshes with clinical ED and ES segmentations from two public
datasets. It stores contour rings, surface samples, signed-distance targets,
and wall-offset targets in one format, allowing the model to be pre-trained on
population shapes and fine-tuned on patient data without changing how the data
are represented. Second, the phase-conditioned model processes endocardial and
epicardial contour information separately, combines a global shape summary
with local contour information, and uses a supervised positive offset to keep
the epicardial field outside the endocardial field. Third, the evaluation
approach assesses how closely reconstructions follow the observed rings, how
much reconstructions from different methods agree, and how results change
when slices are removed. It also compares wall-thickness methods on the same
geometry, then summarises thickness by AHA segment and compares patient
groups. This evaluation approach can be applied to other networks.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:conclusion-limitations}
\noindent
The limitations discussed in \cref{sec:threats} bound all of these answers.
The most important is the absence of an independent 3D reference: the
geometric results measure fidelity to the contours and agreement between
methods, and the thickness results measure reproducibility rather than
accuracy. The pathology cohort is small, and no ablation experiment isolates
the contribution of individual model components.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion-future-work}
\noindent
These limitations set the order of the next steps. The first need is an
anatomical reference. Higher-resolution volumetric acquisitions, or expert
three-dimensional surface and wall-thickness annotations on a larger external
cohort, would turn the agreement figures reported here into accuracy figures
and would show which of the three priors lies closest to the true wall. The
second is a wider and fully matched comparison, adding spline lofting, Poisson
reconstruction and learned alternatives under the same patients, physical
spacing, repair procedure and metrics, with error maps showing where
reconstructions disagree at the apex, the base and the most curved parts of
the wall. The
third is a set of controlled tests that remove
synthetic pre-training, local conditioning, phase conditioning and the
positive-offset coupling one at a time to show which components drive the
model's behaviour. Adding pointwise separation and intersection checks on the
extracted meshes would complete the answer to RQ2. Replacing the binary phase
label with a continuous position in the cardiac cycle would then allow the
representation to be tested for coherence in time as well as continuity in
space.

In sum, this thesis shows that a phase-conditioned implicit representation can
turn a sparse SAX stack into closed, paired LV surfaces that honour the
observed contours, thicken consistently between diastole and systole, and
support locally resolved wall-thickness analysis. It also shows, through the
disagreement between equally plausible reconstructions of the same rings, how
much of the myocardial wall is still inferred rather than observed, and
therefore how carefully any thickness derived from it must be read.
