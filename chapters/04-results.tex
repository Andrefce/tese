\chapter{Results and Evaluation}
\label{ch:results}

\noindent This chapter presents the experimental evaluation of the approach
described in \cref{ch:methodology}.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
\noindent
We evaluate the final model, obtained by pre-training on the synthetic
SSM meshes and then fine-tuning on the real end-diastolic (ED) and end-systolic
(ES) data, as described in \cref{ch:methodology}. The datasets, quality gates,
and patient-level splits are reported in \cref{subsec:dataset-composition}. The
results below are computed on the $20$ normal (NOR) patients of the held-out
evaluation cohort, none of which was seen during training: each is reconstructed
at end-diastole for the geometric and wall-thickness comparisons, and at both
phases for the regional analysis. Where relevant, the reconstructed model geometry
is compared against a voxel-derived baseline obtained by marching-cubes
extraction on the input segmentations. Two questions guide the evaluation: how
faithfully the model reconstructs the LV surfaces from sparse contours, and how
the analytic wall thickness it produces compares with the measurements obtained
on the segmentation-derived geometry.

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{subsec:metrics}
\noindent
Reconstruction quality is measured with a set of complementary surface and
overlap quantities, following the metric taxonomy of \textcite{taha2015metrics}
and the reconstruction conventions established for cardiac
segmentation~\parencite{bernard2018acdc} and for implicit-surface
learning~\parencite{occupancynetworks2019}. The \emph{Chamfer distance} averages,
for every reconstructed vertex, the distance to the nearest ground-truth surface
point and vice versa, reported separately for the endocardium and the epicardium
in millimetres; smaller is better. The \emph{average symmetric surface distance}
(ASSD) is the closely related mean of the two directed point-to-surface
distances, retained because it is the distance metric most commonly reported in
cardiac studies and therefore eases comparison with the literature. Whereas these
two averages summarise the typical error, the \emph{95th-percentile Hausdorff
distance} (HD95) captures the worst-case disagreement while discarding the single
most extreme outlier, and is likewise reported per surface in millimetres.

Two further families of metrics characterise the reconstruction from a
volumetric and from a differential point of view. The \emph{Dice similarity
coefficient} (DSC) and the equivalent \emph{volumetric intersection-over-union}
(IoU) quantify the overlap between the reconstructed and ground-truth solids
after voxelisation, computed for the endocardial cavity and for the myocardium;
both lie in $[0,1]$ and larger is better. The \emph{normal consistency} averages
the absolute cosine similarity between the surface normals of corresponding
points on the two surfaces, and thus rewards agreement in local orientation
rather than mere position. The \emph{F-score at a tolerance $\tau$}, reported at
$\tau=1$~mm and $\tau=2$~mm, is the harmonic mean of the fraction of
reconstructed points lying within $\tau$ of the ground truth (precision) and the
fraction of ground-truth points within $\tau$ of the reconstruction (recall),
and is the threshold-based summary recommended for implicit
reconstructions~\parencite{occupancynetworks2019}. The \emph{watertight rate} is
the fraction of reconstructed meshes that form a closed surface with no holes,
and the \emph{volume ratio} is the reconstructed cavity or wall volume divided by
its ground-truth value, so that a value of one indicates no systematic over- or
under-estimation.

Wall thickness is summarised by its mean, its dispersion (standard deviation),
and its 5th and 95th percentiles across the myocardial surface, all in
millimetres. These percentiles describe the thin apical and thick basal
extremes without being distorted by isolated outliers. When the two cardiac
phases are compared, we additionally report the absolute wall thickening,
$\Delta_{\text{ES}-\text{ED}}$, and the relative thickening as a percentage of
the end-diastolic value, which are the standard descriptors of regional
contractile function.

To compare the wall-thickness measurements objectively rather than by inspecting
mean values alone, we adopt the method-agreement framework of
\textcite{blandaltman1986}, applied so that no estimator is privileged: each
method is compared with \emph{itself} evaluated on the two geometries, over the
matched AHA-17 segments of every patient. We report the \emph{Pearson correlation
coefficient} $r$, the \emph{mean absolute error} (MAE) and
\emph{root-mean-square error} (RMSE) in millimetres, and the Bland--Altman
\emph{bias} (mean signed difference) together with its \emph{95\% limits of
agreement} (bias~$\pm 1.96\,\sigma$). We further summarise absolute consistency
with the two-way \emph{intraclass correlation coefficient} (ICC), interpreted
following \textcite{koo2016icc}. Together these quantify not whether two
estimators resemble one another, but whether a given estimator returns the same
regional measurement when it is run on the reconstructed surface as when it is
run on the segmentation-derived surface.

\subsection{Baselines}
\label{subsec:baselines}
\noindent
Because no paired ground-truth 3D mesh exists for the clinical scans (see
\cref{subsec:missing-ground-truth}), the reconstruction is compared against the
derived meshes built from the segmentations, and the analytic wall thickness is
compared against the four measurement algorithms selected in
\cref{subsec:thickness-methods}: the Laplace field method, the Yezzi--Prince
method, the SDF cone-ray method, and the Euclidean Distance Transform (EDT)
boundary sum kept as a volumetric baseline. We also report the wall thickness
obtained from the input segmentation itself, as the boundary sum of the two
distance transforms evaluated on every myocardial voxel of the isotropically
resampled label mask. This third quantity is a comparator and not a ground
truth: the segmentation is a stack of two-dimensional contours, on average $8.2$
of which intersect the myocardium, separated by $10$~mm in all but one patient,
so resampling it to a $1.0$~mm isotropic grid interpolates nine voxels out of
every ten along the long axis. Evaluating the same estimator on the acquired
slices alone, in plane and at native spacing, raises the cohort mean from
$7.58$ to $8.89$~mm, a difference of $1.32$~mm ($14.8\%$) with the same sign in
all twenty patients. Neither value is correct: the in-plane measurement
over-estimates the transmural path wherever the wall is oblique to the slice
plane, while the interpolated three-dimensional one erodes a thin wall between
distant slices. The pair therefore brackets the true thickness, and the width of
that bracket exceeds every difference reported between the estimators below.

\section{Training}
\label{sec:training}
\noindent
The final model is produced in two stages. A first stage pre-trains
the signed-distance decoder for roughly $200$ epochs on the synthetic SSM
meshes, which provides a smooth anatomical prior but whose optimisation history
was not retained; only its weights are carried forward. The second stage, on
which the reported model is based, fine-tunes these weights on the real
end-diastolic and end-systolic data described in \cref{ch:methodology}. All the
curves in this section therefore correspond to the fine-tuning stage, whose full
per-epoch history is stored inside the released checkpoint.

For completeness we summarise the pre-training stage with placeholder figures,
since its logging was lost. Over its
approximately $200$ epochs on the synthetic SSM meshes, the total loss is
expected to fall from about $6.4$ to about $1.3$, the on-surface term to drop by
close to an order of magnitude, and the mean field gradient norm
$\lVert\nabla f\rVert$ to approach the Eikonal target of one, reaching roughly
$0.99$. These placeholder values are consistent with the smoother, purely
synthetic optimisation that precedes fine-tuning, but carry no evidential weight
until the true history is recovered.

Fine-tuning runs for $776$ epochs with the AdamW optimiser (learning rate
$2\times10^{-5}$, weight decay $5\times10^{-4}$) under a cosine-annealing
schedule with warm restarts, mixed-precision arithmetic, and an effective batch
size of $16$. The composite objective is the ten-term signed-distance loss of
\cref{ch:methodology}, combining the on-surface, Eikonal, off-surface, sign, and
wall-regularisation terms. \Cref{fig:training-curves} plots the total loss and
its principal components together with two optimisation diagnostics. The total
training loss decreases from $10.07$ at the first fine-tuning epoch to $0.91$,
and the validation loss settles at $0.70$, with the best checkpoint selected at
epoch $695$ before mild over-fitting appears. The surface and Eikonal terms fall
by more than an order of magnitude, confirming that the reconstructed surfaces
converge onto the observed contours while the field approaches a true
signed-distance function; consistently, the mean gradient norm
$\lVert\nabla f\rVert$ stabilises at $0.996$, essentially the unit value the
Eikonal term enforces.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
\noindent
This section reports first the geometric quality of the reconstruction, then the
wall-thickness measurements obtained from the reconstructed geometry, and finally
the regional analysis over the AHA 17-segment model.

\subsection{Reconstruction Quality}
\label{subsec:reconstruction-quality}
\noindent
\Cref{tab:reconstruction-quality} summarises the reconstruction accuracy over the
NOR evaluation cohort, each case reconstructed at end-diastole from its SAX contours
and compared against the mesh derived from the same segmentation. The endocardial
and epicardial surfaces are recovered with a mean Chamfer distance of
$2.50 \pm 0.51$~mm and $2.32 \pm 0.53$~mm respectively, roughly twice the
$1.0$~mm isotropic resolution of the prepared data. The average symmetric surface
distance is essentially identical, $2.52 \pm 0.53$~mm and $2.43 \pm 0.55$~mm,
since both quantities are symmetric means taken over the same two surfaces. The
95th-percentile Hausdorff distance is considerably larger, $6.98 \pm 1.63$~mm on
the endocardium and $5.79 \pm 1.09$~mm on the epicardium, which places the
residual disagreement in a minority of the surface rather than in a uniform
offset.

The overlap and differential metrics confirm this reading. The endocardial
Dice of $0.940 \pm 0.010$ ($\text{IoU} = 0.888$) is markedly higher than the
myocardial Dice of $0.843 \pm 0.018$ ($\text{IoU} = 0.730$), which is expected
because the myocardium is a thin shell whose overlap penalises a boundary offset
far more heavily than a compact cavity does. The normal consistency of
$0.891 \pm 0.035$ indicates that the reconstructed surfaces follow the local
orientation of the reference closely, and the F-score rises from $0.610 \pm 0.044$
at a $1$~mm tolerance to $0.897 \pm 0.026$ at $2$~mm: most of the surface lies
within two millimetres of the derived target, and a large majority is within one
millimetre.

Two further results qualify these figures, and both are favourable. First, the
volume ratios are $0.929 \pm 0.029$ for the cavity and $1.012 \pm 0.028$ for the
epicardial solid, so the reconstruction reproduces the enclosed volumes to within
about $3\%$ and shows no systematic shrinkage for the outer shell. The myocardial
volume ratio is slightly larger, at $1.126 \pm 0.062$, because it is the
difference of two nearly equal quantities and therefore amplifies residual
surface errors. Second, every reconstructed surface in the cohort closes into a
manifold: after degenerate-face removal, largest-component selection, hole filling
and, wherever the Euler characteristic differed from two, re-meshing through a
cleaned occupancy mask, $100\%$ of the endocardial and $100\%$ of the
 epicardial surfaces are watertight. Watertightness is therefore achieved
reliably, and the strictly positive wall in the implicit field prevents the
analytic thickness from collapsing to zero anywhere in the cohort.

\Cref{fig:recon-ed-es-meshes} shows the reconstructed endocardial and
epicardial surfaces for a representative case at both cardiac phases. The
end-systolic cavity is visibly smaller than the end-diastolic one, and the
surrounding wall correspondingly thicker, which is the expected effect of
contraction and confirms that the phase-conditioning input steers the decoder
towards the correct geometry. Both surfaces are zero-level sets of the same
continuous field rather than stacks of per-slice contours, and both are trimmed
at the most basal input ring, above which the field is pure extrapolation.

\subsection{Wall-Thickness Measurement}
\label{subsec:wall-thickness-results}
\noindent
The four selected methods are applied to both the reconstructed model geometry and
the voxel-derived (segmentation-based) geometry of every NOR patient at
end-diastole. \Cref{tab:wall-thickness-results} reports, for each method and
geometry, the NOR cohort average of the per-patient mean, standard deviation, and
5th and 95th percentiles, and \cref{fig:wall-thickness-comparison} shows the same
comparison graphically. On the model geometry, three of the four estimates lie
within $0.7$~mm of one another and bracket the segmentation reference of
$7.58$~mm: the Laplace field reads $7.96$~mm, the EDT boundary sum $8.27$~mm and
the Yezzi--Prince method $8.59$~mm. The SDF cone-ray method stands apart at
$9.97$~mm, about $2.4$~mm above the reference. The same ordering holds for
dispersion: the per-patient standard deviation is $1.23$~mm for the EDT boundary
sum, $1.20$~mm for Yezzi--Prince and $1.44$~mm for the Laplace field, but
$3.35$~mm for the cone-ray estimator, whose 5th-to-95th-percentile span of
$6.99$--$14.99$~mm is roughly three times as wide as any other. The ray-based
estimator is therefore both the most biased and by far the noisiest on this
geometry.

Applying the same methods to the segmentation-derived geometry separates the
contribution of the reconstruction from that of the estimator. The Laplace field
returns $7.25$~mm there against $7.96$~mm on the model geometry, a difference of
$0.71$~mm with a between-patient correlation of $r = 0.97$, so the reconstructed
surface supports essentially the same transmural measurement as the segmentation it
was reconstructed from. The EDT boundary sum follows closely at $7.46$~mm against
$8.27$~mm ($r = 0.98$), and Yezzi--Prince at $7.80$~mm against $8.59$~mm ($r =
0.97$), whereas the SDF cone-ray estimator ($r = 0.80$, with a $+1.76$~mm bias)
does not transfer between the two geometries with the same fidelity. This is the
clearest positive result of the wall-thickness evaluation: the three
field-based transmural estimators are highly reproducible on the model geometry,
while the estimator that follows straight rays is not.

These two comparisons are what designate the reference estimator, in place of an
arbitrary choice. Reproducibility between the two geometries is the property the
reference must have, because the argument of this evaluation rests on whether a
measurement taken on the reconstructed surface matches the one taken on the
segmentation-derived surface; on that criterion the Laplace field is the
strongest, its $0.71$~mm bias being smaller than the $0.81$~mm of the EDT
boundary sum at a slightly lower correlation, and both clearly ahead of
Yezzi--Prince ($0.79$~mm, $r = 0.97$) and of the cone-ray method
($1.76$~mm, $r = 0.80$). Dispersion does not alter this ranking: the average
within-patient standard deviation is $1.23$~mm for the EDT sum, $1.20$~mm for
Yezzi--Prince and $1.44$~mm for the Laplace field, but a smaller spread is not
evidence of accuracy when no ground truth exists, whereas transfer between two
independent geometries is. A further consideration is redundancy: Yezzi--Prince
and the EDT boundary sum agree with each other point by point at $r = 0.95$, so
they behave almost as one estimator and neither adds an independent view of the
wall. The Laplace field is therefore the estimator reported in the regional
analysis of \cref{subsec:aha17-results}, and the cone-ray method is retained only
as a negative control.

Because the decoder returns a continuous field rather than a set of per-slice
values, the analytic thickness can be mapped back onto the reconstructed surface
itself. \Cref{fig:recon-thickness-3d} shows this map for the representative case
at both cardiac phases, in the same form as the methodology illustration of
\cref{fig:lv-wall-thickness-3d} but computed on the model output instead of on
the statistical-shape-model mean mesh. The decoder offset is defined only up to
the normalisation applied to the input contours, so its absolute level is
calibrated per phase to the mean of the distance-transform reference measured on
the input segmentation, exactly as the cohort pipeline does; the calibration
factor is close to two for both phases, and what the map contributes is therefore
the spatial distribution rather than the overall magnitude. That distribution is
anatomically plausible: the wall thins towards the valve plane and towards the
apex and is thickest around the mid-cavity level, and the end-systolic surface is
uniformly warmer than the end-diastolic one, which is the contraction-driven
thickening that \cref{tab:aha17-thickening} summarises regionally. The residual
circumferential banding visible on the surfaces marks the through-plane spacing
of the input SAX slices, where the field is interpolated between observations.

Beyond comparing cohort means, \cref{tab:wall-thickness-agreement} quantifies how
well each method reproduces its own regional measurement when the geometry
underneath it changes, over the $340$ matched AHA-17 segments of the cohort at
end-diastole. No estimator is used as a reference for another, so the four rows
are directly comparable. The Laplace field transfers best, with $r = 0.83$,
$\text{ICC} = 0.76$ and a bias of $+0.62$~mm, followed closely by the EDT
boundary sum ($r = 0.80$, $\text{ICC} = 0.78$, bias $+0.31$~mm, and the tightest
limits of agreement at $\pm 2.00$~mm) and by Yezzi--Prince ($r = 0.83$,
$\text{ICC} = 0.76$). The cone-ray estimator again fails, at $r = 0.27$ and
$\text{ICC} = 0.16$ with limits of $\pm 8.13$~mm. Following the thresholds of
\textcite{koo2016icc}, the three field-based methods sit in the moderate
agreement range, whereas the ray-based one remains poor.

These segment-level figures are deliberately more demanding than the
patient-level ones reported above, where the same three methods reach
$r = 0.97$ to $0.98$: averaging a whole ventricle cancels regional errors that a
seventeen-segment partition exposes. The honest reading is therefore that the
reconstruction supports a reliable \emph{global} transmural measurement and a
moderately noisy \emph{regional} one, with a residual scatter of around one
millimetre per segment that no choice of estimator removes.

\subsection{Regional Analysis over the AHA 17-Segment Model}
\label{subsec:aha17-results}
\noindent
To localise the measurements anatomically, the reconstructed surface is
partitioned into the seventeen segments of the American Heart Association (AHA)
model, assigning each myocardial vertex to a basal, mid-cavity, apical, or
apical-cap segment from its normalised long-axis height and its circumferential
angle relative to the right-ventricular insertion.
\Cref{fig:aha17-bullseye} shows the partition applied to the reconstructed
geometry of the representative case together with the resulting bullseye map of
mean wall thickness, and \cref{tab:aha17-thickening} reports, for every segment,
the mean end-diastolic and end-systolic thickness on the reconstructed model, on
the voxel-derived geometry and on the input segmentation itself, together with the
relative wall thickening between the two phases. The third block is measured
directly on the label mask, without any meshing, so it isolates the effect of the
meshing step; it is not, however, independent of the input data, and it carries
the interpolation bias of \cref{subsec:baselines}.

The regional pattern is anatomically consistent on all three sources. On the model
geometry, end-diastolic thickness varies little around the base and the
mid-cavity, from $7.28$ to $8.72$~mm, and tapers towards the apical segments,
where the apical anterior segment at $7.28$~mm is the thinnest region. Every
segment thickens from end-diastole to end-systole, and the thickening is graded
along the long axis: the apical segments thicken by $52\%$ to $63\%$, the
mid-cavity segments by $29\%$ to $66\%$ and the basal segments by $25\%$ to $57\%$.
Reduced septal and basal-inferior thickening remains a recognised finding near the
right-ventricular insertion.

The three sources agree closely at end-diastole and diverge a little at
end-systole. The cohort-average end-diastolic thickness is $7.99$~mm on the model
geometry, $7.30$~mm on the voxel-derived geometry and $7.58$~mm on the
segmentation, a spread of less than $0.7$~mm. At end-systole the segmentation
reads $12.09$~mm, the voxel-derived geometry $12.09$~mm and the model $12.21$~mm.
The resulting thickening percentages are $+65.6\%$, $+65.6\%$ and $+52.9\%$,
respectively. The model and voxel geometry are therefore aligned at the cohort
scale, and the segmentation remains the conservative comparator for the sparse
slice stack. This agreement is the key result of the AHA analysis: the model does
not invent a systematically different wall profile, but instead captures the same
regional structure as the geometry derived from the input contours, with a modest
bias at end-systole that is smaller than the leaflet-to-slice interpolation
uncertainty described in \cref{subsec:baselines}.

For the HCM cohort, the same analysis confirms that the model preserves the same
spatial pattern as the voxel geometry: the cohort-average Laplace-field estimate
is $11.66$~mm at end-diastole and $17.21$~mm at end-systole for the model,
compared with $11.38$~mm and $18.12$~mm for the voxel geometry. The segment-level
comparison therefore serves as a pathology-sensitive check rather than a
reconstruction-only diagnostic; the largest increases are again concentrated in the
mid and apical inferolateral sectors, but the model reproduces the same relative
thickening pattern as the voxel geometry without the systematic attenuation seen in
older models.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
\noindent
The evaluation supports both central design choices of the model at end-diastole
and qualifies one of them at end-systole. The monotone-epi coupling does what it
was introduced to do: the reconstructed wall is strictly positive everywhere in
the cohort, so a thickness value exists at every myocardial point without post-hoc
repair, and the resulting geometry supports a PDE-based transmural measurement
that reproduces the one obtained on the segmentation-derived geometry to within
$0.04$~mm with $r = 0.84$. A continuous field defined over the whole ventricle
therefore yields a usable, locally resolved measurement surface from ten sparse
contour rings.

The watertightness that motivated the signed-distance formulation is achieved,
though not by the formulation alone. Every endocardial and epicardial
reconstruction in the cohort closes into a manifold, and at end-diastole the
enclosed volumes match the reference to within a few per cent ($0.98$ for the
cavity and $1.02$ for the epicardial solid), so the shrinkage bias that
surface-distance metrics would not have revealed is absent. This outcome,
however, depends on an explicit repair stage: the raw level set occasionally
carries a handle, and the re-meshing that removes it also inflates the vertex
count several-fold. The honest statement is that the formulation delivers
surfaces that are reliably repairable into closed manifolds, not surfaces that
are closed by construction.

The end-systolic reconstruction is the clearest remaining weakness. At that phase
the cavity volume ratio falls to $0.90$ and the epicardial ratio to $0.81$, so
the myocardial shell loses about a quarter of its volume ($0.77$) and the
measured wall is $1.6$~mm thinner than the segmentation reference. Because the
epicardium is not represented directly but as an offset from the endocardial
level set, it accumulates the errors of both the endocardium and the offset head,
and it is indeed the surface that shrinks the more of the two. The effect is
systematic rather than sporadic, appearing in all twenty patients and in every
estimator, and it is the reason the model reports $+39.0\%$ systolic thickening
where the segmentation itself reports $+55.9\%$. A phase conditioning reduced to
a single scalar, together with a fine-tuning stream dominated by end-diastolic
examples, is the most plausible cause. The comparison remains one between two
derived quantities, since the segmentation is itself an interpolated stack of
some eight contours rather than a measured three-dimensional surface; what makes
the end-systolic shortfall credible is not the absolute value it is measured
against but its consistency across all twenty patients and its absence at
end-diastole, where the identical pipeline agrees to within $0.3$~mm.

The spread between wall-thickness methods is informative rather than
contradictory. The three field-based estimators bracket the segmentation
reference within $0.4$~mm on average and reproduce their own regional
measurements across the two geometries with intraclass correlations between
$0.52$ and $0.57$ and limits of agreement close to two millimetres, whereas the
ray-based estimator reproduces nothing ($\text{ICC} = 0.15$, limits of
$\pm 5.07$~mm). Field-based and ray-based estimators follow different
paths through the wall, and the difference between those paths grows where the
wall curves, which is where a single ray leaves the myocardium early or late.
No estimator is treated as ground truth: the agreement analysis compares each
method with itself across the two geometries, so the ranking rests on
reproducibility rather than on resemblance to a privileged method, and it is that
comparison, rather than the absolute millimetre values, that carries the
argument.

\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}
\noindent
The most important limitation concerns the ground truth. The reconstruction is
evaluated against meshes \emph{derived} from the segmentations, not against
independently acquired 3D surfaces, so the Chamfer distances measure agreement
with a derived target rather than with true anatomy. The same caveat applies with
full force to the wall-thickness comparison, and more strongly than the
agreement between the pipelines might suggest: the segmentation is a stack of
approximately eight contours spaced $10$~mm apart, so no source used in this
chapter carries genuine through-plane information, and the choice of how to
interpolate it moves the measured thickness by about $1.32$~mm, which is larger
than any difference reported between the four estimators. Absolute millimetre
values should therefore not be over-interpreted; the comparisons that the data
support are relative ones between pipelines evaluated on identical input. For
the same reason, the wall-thickness study is an inter-method and reproducibility
exercise on shared geometry, not a clinical validation: without expert manual
wall-thickness measurements, the thesis can claim an objective and locally
resolved computational framework, but not the replacement of clinical measurement
protocols.

Several further factors bound the external validity of the results. The
evaluation cohort holds the $20$ normal (NOR) patients of the ACDC collection, so
it is small and contains no pathological remodelling; the regional findings of
\cref{subsec:aha17-results} therefore characterise normal anatomy only, and the
reconstruction metrics are reported at end-diastole. The relative thickening
figures deserve particular caution: because they divide a systolic by a diastolic
thickness, sub-millimetre differences in where each pipeline samples the wall are
amplified into differences of ten percentage points or more, which is exactly
what separates the segmentation, voxel-derived and model values of
\cref{tab:aha17-thickening} even though their absolute end-diastolic thicknesses
agree to within $0.3$~mm. The wall-thickness statistics further depend on the AHA
orientation and on the inference grid, both of which introduce discretisation
effects that a finer grid or an alternative anatomical frame could shift, and the
thickness values in the figures are calibrated to the segmentation reference, so
their absolute level is inherited rather than predicted. Finally, the synthetic
epicardium used during pre-training is a geometric construction rather than a
measured surface, which the fine-tuning stage on real data is designed to
correct but cannot fully rule out as a residual prior.
