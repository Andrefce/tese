\chapter{Literature Review}
\label{ch:literature-review}

\noindent This chapter surveys the existing literature relevant to left-ventricle
reconstruction from short-axis cardiac MRI. We focus on three connected themes: the
problem of reconstructing the left ventricle from short-axis magnetic
resonance images, the use of statistical shape models to regularize cardiac
geometry, and point-based implicit representations that reconstruct smooth
surfaces from sparse contour observations.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}
\noindent
Short-axis cardiac magnetic resonance imaging is widely used because it offers
clear anatomical contrast and standardized coverage of the left ventricle, but
the imaging geometry remains fundamentally slice-based rather than volumetric
\parencite{petitjean2011segmentationreview,cerqueira2002segmentation}. This
creates a reconstruction problem: the left ventricle must be inferred in three
dimensions from a stack of two-dimensional contours or partial surfaces, and the
quality of the result depends on how well the method can interpolate between
slices while preserving global shape and local wall geometry
\parencite{heimann2009ssmreview}.

The literature on cardiac shape analysis makes a consistent distinction between
methods that learn a compact representation of shape and methods that directly
operate on image or mesh topology. Compact representations are attractive
because they reduce the effect of noise and missing data, but they also impose a
limited deformation space. Direct methods are more expressive, but they tend to
require more data and stronger inductive bias to remain anatomically plausible
\parencite{suinesiaputra2014collaborative,bai2015biventricularatlas,heimann2009ssmreview}.

Statistical shape models are therefore a natural bridge between the imaging
problem and the downstream geometric analysis. In the cardiac setting, they are
used to encode variation across subjects, to regularize segmentation, and to
derive measurements such as ventricular volume or wall thickness
\parencite{heimann2009ssmreview,suinesiaputra2018infarctchallenge}.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}
\noindent
The literature falls into three families. Early work
on left-ventricle reconstruction focused on interpolation and parametric
surface fitting, later work emphasized statistical shape models, and more recent
approaches adopt point-set encoders and implicit surface representations to
model geometry at continuous resolution \parencite{heimann2009ssmreview,
petitjean2011segmentationreview,qi2017pointnet,deepsdf2019,
occupancynetworks2019,tancik2020fourierfeatures}.

\subsection{Interpolation and classical shape fitting}
\noindent
Classical reconstruction methods usually begin with a set of segmented SAX
slices and then interpolate between them or fit a parametric model to the
observed contours. Reviews of cardiac segmentation note that such methods are
attractive because they are computationally simple, but their performance is
limited by the absence of direct information between slices, especially near the
apex and base of the ventricle \parencite{petitjean2011segmentationreview,
heimann2009ssmreview}. In practice, these methods tend to encode geometric
assumptions explicitly, so their accuracy depends on how closely those
assumptions match the anatomy of the patient being reconstructed
\parencite{heimann2009ssmreview}.

The main advantage of this family is interpretability. When a reconstruction is
driven by interpolation or by a low-order geometric prior, failures are easier
to inspect and debug than in a purely learned pipeline. The main drawback is
that the model does not learn a rich data-driven representation of cardiac
variation, so it may underfit subjects whose ventricle geometry departs from the
training assumptions \parencite{heimann2009ssmreview,bai2015biventricularatlas}.

\subsection{Statistical shape models}
\noindent
Statistical shape models provide a compact representation of anatomical
variation by aligning a training set of meshes or contours and then learning the
dominant modes of variation, typically through principal component analysis
\parencite{cootes1995asm,heimann2009ssmreview}. For the left ventricle, this
framework is especially useful because it can absorb missing slices, suppress
segmentation noise, and constrain the reconstructed surface to remain within a
plausible cardiac manifold \parencite{suinesiaputra2014collaborative}.

Cardiac-specific studies have shown that statistical atlases and shape models can
be built from large cohorts and used to study normal variation, disease-related
remodelling, and motion patterns \parencite{medrano2014lvshapevariation,
bai2015biventricularatlas,khalafvand2018lvflowssm}. In particular, the left
ventricle does not vary only by size; it also varies by eccentricity, curvature,
and regional deformation, which makes the choice of shape basis important for
later measurements \parencite{medrano2014lvshapevariation,
suinesiaputra2018infarctchallenge}.

The main limitation of a linear statistical model is that it compresses the
population into a low-dimensional subspace. This is useful when the goal is
regularization, but it can become restrictive when the anatomy is complex or
when the input slices are sparse \parencite{heimann2009ssmreview,bai2015biventricularatlas}.

\subsection{Point-based and implicit representation learning}
\noindent
PointNet introduced a simple but effective idea for unordered geometric data:
an encoder can consume a point set directly if the architecture is permutation
invariant and aggregates local features into a global latent code
\parencite{qi2017pointnet}. This matches the implemented notebook pipeline,
which starts from
contour-derived point samples and learns a latent shape description before
decoding geometry.

The decoder side of the model belongs to the implicit surface family. DeepSDF
represents a shape as the zero level set of a learned signed-distance function,
which makes completion from partial input and shape interpolation natural
\parencite{deepsdf2019}. Occupancy Networks use a closely related idea, but
predict occupancy instead of signed distance; the common point is that the
surface is reconstructed as a continuous decision boundary rather than as a
fixed polygonal template \parencite{occupancynetworks2019}.

The inspected training notebook clarifies the implemented practical direction.
The model is explicitly phase-conditioned and predicts
paired endocardial and epicardial signed-distance fields with a coupled
parameterization that preserves positive wall thickness by construction. In the
same implementation, Fourier feature mappings and SDF-oriented losses
concentrate supervision on surface fidelity, gradient regularity, and
off-surface consistency, while mesh extraction is performed from the zero level
set with marching cubes. This design is consistent with implicit SDF
reconstruction literature
\parencite{qi2017pointnet,deepsdf2019,occupancynetworks2019,tancik2020fourierfeatures}.

Fourier feature mappings are relevant because they mitigate the spectral bias of
plain multilayer perceptrons and help them fit high-frequency geometric detail
\parencite{tancik2020fourierfeatures}. In practice, this matters when the model
must preserve thin walls, sharp curvature changes, or small local variations in
the reconstructed ventricle surface.

In the implemented pipeline, the key point is the combination of a
PointNet-style encoder with an implicit SDF decoder. That combination is more
consistent with the actual notebooks because it learns a compact latent code
from unordered samples and decodes a continuous surface that can be
interrogated at arbitrary resolution
\parencite{qi2017pointnet,deepsdf2019,tancik2020fourierfeatures}.

\subsection{Wall-thickness analysis}
\noindent
Wall thickness is a clinically meaningful quantity because it reflects cardiac
remodelling and myocardial health, and it is typically measured as the distance
between endocardial and epicardial surfaces or through equivalent geometric
constructs \parencite{grossman1974wallthickness,cerqueira2002segmentation}.
Recent MRI studies continue to treat wall thickness as a sensitive marker of
myocardial structure and tissue properties \parencite{huelnhagen2017t2wallthickness}.

The literature therefore links the reconstruction problem to a downstream
measurement problem. A method that reconstructs the left ventricle more
faithfully will usually yield more stable thickness estimates, whereas a method
that over-smooths the surface can attenuate local thickness variation
\parencite{grossman1974wallthickness,huelnhagen2017t2wallthickness}. This is why
wall-thickness estimation cannot be treated as a detached post-processing step:
it depends on the geometric fidelity of the reconstructed surface itself
\parencite{cerqueira2002segmentation,medrano2014lvshapevariation}.

\section{Summary and Research Gap}
\label{sec:research-gap}
\noindent
The reviewed literature shows a clear progression. Classical interpolation and
parametric fitting methods are simple but limited. Statistical shape models add
a strong anatomical prior and are well suited to cardiac anatomy, but they are
restricted by the learned latent space. Point-based implicit methods offer
continuous surface modelling from sparse observations, yet they require careful
sampling and regularization to remain anatomically stable
\parencite{heimann2009ssmreview,bai2015biventricularatlas,qi2017pointnet,
deepsdf2019,tancik2020fourierfeatures}.

The identified gap is the combination of these strengths in a
single pipeline. Existing studies usually emphasize either population-level
shape priors or generic implicit reconstruction, but less often a unified
workflow that starts from sparse SAX contours, conditions reconstruction on
cardiac phase, and preserves downstream thickness consistency as part of model
design \parencite{suinesiaputra2018infarctchallenge,grossman1974wallthickness,
huelnhagen2017t2wallthickness,qi2017pointnet,deepsdf2019}. The remaining chapters
describe a pipeline that combines statistical shape modelling,
PointNet-style encoding, phase-conditioned implicit SDF decoding, and
wall-thickness-oriented evaluation.
